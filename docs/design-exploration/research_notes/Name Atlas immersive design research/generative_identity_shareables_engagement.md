# Generative identity systems, shareable personal-data artifacts, data-story narrative structures and ethical engagement — research notes for Name Atlas

Researched 2026-09-30. Scope: evidence and examples for (1) how a surname string could deterministically seed an honest, reproducible visual "signature", (2) what makes personal-data artifacts shareable, (3) which narrative structure fits a 60–120 s mobile discovery, (4) how to create engagement ethically (including an honest paid offer).

**Verification legend.** **[V]** = verified in this session by fetching the primary/official page or PDF (Wikipedia pages are marked as such). **[S]** = taken from search-result summaries/snippets only, not independently fetched. Treat [S] as lower confidence and re-check before quoting it publicly. Throughout, **"Cited Findings" = FACT** (as reported by the source) and **"Inferences" = INTERPRETATION** by this researcher.

## 1. Generative and dynamic identity systems: how do they balance recognisability with variation?

### Takeaway
Every durable dynamic identity examined pairs one fixed, recognisable **constant** with a **variable** layer. The constant is a grid, a frame, a silhouette, a line-logic or a wordmark. The variable layer is driven by a declared, honest input: live weather data, a source image's colours, real student artwork, a research group or an individual. Recognition lives in the constant; meaning and personalisation live in the variable. The MIT Media Lab moved from ~40,000 algorithmic per-person marks (2011) to one 7×7 grid with 23 bounded group glyphs (2014). That suggests unbounded variation needs a much stronger family constant.

### Cited Findings
**MIT Media Lab, 2011 (TheGreenEyl: E Roon Kang and Richard The)**
- The algorithmic identity used 3 shapes and 12 colours, for "40,000 possible variations total". An algorithm generated a unique logo for each staff member through a custom web interface, combining personal and organisational branding. — [V] [The Next Web, 10 Mar 2011](https://thenextweb.com/news/mit-media-labs-new-logo-has-40000-variations)
- Designer's concept (verbatim): "Each of the three shapes stands for one individual's contribution, the resulting shape represents the outcome of this process: A constant redefinition of what media and technology means today." — [V] [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/mit-media-labs-new-logo-has-40000-variations)
- On recognisability, commentator Duane Kinsey is quoted saying that although every staff business card carries a different mark, "there's no confusing the source". — [V] [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/mit-media-labs-new-logo-has-40000-variations)
- The identity was built in Processing and made for the Lab's 25th anniversary. Search summaries describe it as "40,000 logos in 12 color combinations (~480,000 total)". — [S] [CreativeApplications.net](https://www.creativeapplications.net/project/mit-media-lab-identity-processing/); [S] [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/thegreeneyl-mit-media-lab-identity). **Conflict:** TNW says 40,000 variations *in total*, while the search summary multiplies by 12 colour combinations (~480,000).

**MIT Media Lab, 2014 (Pentagram, partner Michael Bierut)**
- The project "began with designer Richard The's 25th anniversary logo for the Media Lab, which was based on a seven-by-seven grid." "Using the same underlying grid, the designers extended that identity to each of the 23 research groups that lie at the heart of the Lab's activity." — [V] [Pentagram](https://www.pentagram.com/work/mit-media-lab)
- "The result is an interrelated system of glyphs that at once establishes a fixed identity for the Media Lab, but celebrates the diversity of activity that makes the Lab great." Helvetica "has been reinstated to support the overall system." — [V] [Pentagram](https://www.pentagram.com/work/mit-media-lab)
- The Pentagram page does not mention the 40,000-variation predecessor or why it was replaced. — [V] [Pentagram](https://www.pentagram.com/work/mit-media-lab)

**Visit Nordkyn (Neue, Norway)**
- The logo "changes according to wind and temperature", in the same way the area changes with the weather. Neue quotes Brad Flowers (Forbes): "It is part logo, part useful information, part art. It is the intersection of design and technology." — [V] [Neue](https://neue.no/work/visit-nordkyn/)
- Further details from search summaries:
  - The logo is inspired by a snowflake shape and the strapline "Where nature rules".

  - Magenta and turquoise indicate a rise and fall in temperature.
  - A generator lets the client download the logo for the exact weather at a given moment.
  - It won a D&AD Wood Pencil (2011, Branding).
  - [S] [LS:N Global](https://lsnglobal.com/article/view/2752); [S] [Web Directions](https://webdirections.org/blog/design-inspiration-long-live-the-api/); [S] [D&AD](https://www.dandad.org/awards/professional/2011/branding/18295/where-nature-rules/)

**Casa da Música (Sagmeister Inc., Porto, 2007)**
- The identity is derived from Rem Koolhaas's building: six building profiles with 17 planes serve as logo variations. A colour tool samples an existing image (for example, a painting of Beethoven) to pull 17 colours that fill the facets. — [S] [Cooper Hewitt collection record](https://www-4.collection.cooperhewitt.org/objects/18794271). - For the I/O 2026 "Save the Date", Gemini models generated game levels and boards. — [S] [Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/how-we-built-the-google-io-2026-save-the-date-experience/)

**Analytical framework**
- Martins, Cunha, Bicker and Machado propose a model for analysing dynamic visual identities. It separates the *variation mechanisms* used from the *features achieved*, and aims to help designers with needs such as giving the public "an active role". — [S] [Visible Language](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/4649)

### Inferences
- **Constant + variable + declared input** is the shared grammar:

  | Identity | Constant | Variable | Input |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | MIT 2014 | 7×7 grid | glyph per group | research group |
  | OCAD U | frame/windows | artwork | real student work |
  | Nordkyn | snowflake form | shape and colour | weather data |
  | Casa da Música | building silhouette | facet colours | a sampled image |
  | Whitney | zigzag logic | shape | the surrounding layout |
  | Melbourne | the M | pattern and colour | the programme |

- **The input's honesty is part of the identity's credibility.** Nordkyn is explicitly "part useful information": viewers are invited to *read data into the mark*. By the same logic, users will read facts into any Name Atlas signature that changes with data. If the variable layer were driven by anything other than the letters (for example region, age or "meaning"), the art would silently become a claim.
- **How much variation?** MIT went from per-person variation (tens of thousands of marks) back to a bounded family on one grid. Name Atlas *needs* per-surname uniqueness, so its constant must be correspondingly strong: one grid or frame, one typeface, one fixed grammar.
- **Participation creates ownership.** OCAD and Casa da Música let people or content drive the variable. In Name Atlas, the user's own act of typing the surname can be the participatory input.
- **Stability.** A data-driven mark like Nordkyn lives in the moment. A surname does not change, so a name-driven mark should be immutable, which helps recognition and share consistency.

### Gaps
- No user studies found that quantify recognisability versus variation for dynamic identities. Everything above comes from designers and critics.
- The rationale for MIT's 2011→2014 change is not documented in the fetched sources.
- Nordkyn's data-update frequency and exact visual mapping were not verified; Neue's page lacks specifics.
- Casa da Música is sourced only from a Cooper Hewitt snippet, and Sagmeister's page returned 404.
- No evidence found of a formal, code-generated Google I/O identity system beyond the 2013 interactive logo experiment.
- The publication year and issue of Martins et al. were not verified (the search summary said 2021).

## 2. Deterministic generative art from a seed/hash: what makes the output feel personal and beautiful rather than random?

### Takeaway
Three things separate a personal-feeling generative mark from a random one:
- **Determinism** (same seed → same output, forever) is the technical basis for ownership.
- **Beauty at scale** comes from designing the *whole distribution* of outputs (curated palettes, probability-weighted features, collision rules, QA across thousands of outputs), not from lucky individual outputs.
- **Personal meaning** appears when the mapping from input to form is *legible*. Musical cryptograms have been meaningful for centuries because the key is known; an opaque hash such as an identicon only distinguishes users.

The robust name-letter effect is a plausible, though untested for art, reason why letter-derived forms may feel "mine".

### Cited Findings
- **Art Blocks.** Each token has `tokenData.hash`, a 32-byte hex seed that is "your only randomness source". Artists must not use `Math.random()` or `Date.now()`. Instead they seed a PRNG (the docs show sfc32) from the hash, so the same hash always yields the same output. — [S] [Art Blocks docs](https://docs.artblocks.io/creator-onboarding/artists/1-building-your-project)
- **Tyler Hobbs, "The Rise of Long-Form Generative Art" (6 Aug 2021)** — [V] [tylerxhobbs.com](https://tylerxhobbs.com/essays/2021/the-rise-of-long-form-generative-art)
  - Edition sizes are "typically 500-1000". At mint, "the script is run to generate a new output, and that output is wrapped in an NFT and transferred to the collector."
  - "With long-form works, the artist has nowhere to hide, and collectors will get to know the scope of the algorithm almost as well as the artist."
  - Artists once accepted "95% garbage and 5% gems". Now "bad results are extremely rare. The algorithm can't stumble across great results by luck anymore, it needs to produce them dependably."
  - The goal is "consistent quality and high variety, while maintaining the existing need for unity across all output"; "every output will have something new about it, a little surprise"; the collection should be "a unified whole, something that is the full expression of a single idea."
  - Hobbs spent about two months on quality assurance for *Fidenza*.
- **Fidenza** (Art Blocks project #78, released 11 Jun 2021) — [V] [Tyler Hobbs – Fidenza](https://www.tylerxhobbs.com/words/fidenza)
  - "The main basis of *Fidenza* is a flow field algorithm". Curves are non-overlapping and collision-checked.
  - Traits include:
    - Scale (7 variants)
    - Turbulence (None/Low/Medium/High)
    - Stroke styles (Super Blocks/Outline/Soft Shapes)
    - Sharp angles and Spiral modes
    - Collision modes (No Overlap/Relaxed/Anything Goes)
    - Margin
  - There are "14 different probabilistic color palettes"; Luxe (16 colours) is the most common.
  - Hobbs: "striking the right balance of unpredictability and quality is a difficult challenge".
- **Identicons** were invented by Don Park on 18 Jan 2007: "I originally came up with this idea to be used as an easy means of visually distinguishing multiple units of information, anything that can be reduced to bits." They typically visualise a hash of an IP address. — [V] [Wikipedia: Identicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identicon)
- **GitHub identicons (14 Aug 2013)** — [V] [GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/2013-08-14-identicons/)
  - They are 5×5 "pixel" sprites "generated using a hash of the user's ID". The algorithm "walks through the hash and turns pixels on or off depending on even or odd values".
  - "These generated patterns, combined with hash-determined color values, ensures a huge number of unique Identicons." They are used for users without a Gravatar.
- **Boring Avatars** is "an open source React library and a subscription-based service that generates unique SVG-based user profile avatars from usernames, emails or any random strings". Palettes are passed in as props; the sample palettes come from Matt DesLauriers's "Nice Color Palettes". — [V] [boringavatars.com](https://boringavatars.com/)
- **Musical cryptograms (name → music)** — [V] [Wikipedia: Musical cryptogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_cryptogram)
  - German note names make many letters playable: B = B♭, H = B♮, "Es" (E♭) = S, "As" (A♭).
  - Examples: the B-A-C-H motif; Schumann's *Abegg Variations* (dated 1829 by Wikipedia) on A–B♭–E–G–G for Meta Abegg; and Shostakovich's D-S-C-H motif (D–E♭–C–B♮), "appearing in many of his works".
  - A French method from the late 19th century maps every letter onto A–G, many-to-one.
- **Name-letter effect** — [V] [Wikipedia: Name-letter effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Name-letter_effect)
  - It is "the tendency of people to prefer the letters in their name over other letters in the alphabet".
  - In Nuttin's data, the probability of a letter being chosen among one's six preferred letters was 0.30 for name letters versus 0.20 for other letters, and 0.46 for initials.
  - It has been tested in 15 languages across four language families and five alphabets.
  - The broader "implicit egotism" claims about life decisions are contested (Simonsohn).
- **Unicode UAX #15.** A precomposed character and the equivalent base letter + combining mark sequence are canonically equivalent. Only after normalization do "two equivalent strings... have precisely the same normalized form", which is required for reliable comparison and hashing. — [V] [Unicode UAX #15](https://unicode.org/reports/tr15/)

### Inferences
- **What makes a mark "personal" rather than "random":**
  1. **Reproducibility.** It is the same every visit and on every device, so it can be *owned* and recognised.
  2. **Legibility of the mapping.** The user can *see their letters* in it. Musical cryptograms are the precedent: meaning comes from a known key, whereas identicons are opaque by design.
  3. **Curated constraint.** Hobbs's long-form principle: design the distribution so that *every* output is good, using palettes, weighted features and QA over the whole population.
  4. **"A little surprise".** Each output needs one distinctive feature, but within a unified family.
- **Hash avalanche is a double-edged sword** (general property of cryptographic hashes; not separately sourced here). Near-identical inputs such as *Kowalski/Kowalska* or *Nowak/Nowakowski* hash to unrelated values. That is good for uniqueness but bad for family resemblance and for the user's sense that the art "comes from my letters". A **two-layer design** addresses this:
  - (a) a *structural layer* mapped transparently from the letters, so related surnames visibly share a stem;
  - (b) a *hash-seeded ornamental layer* (grain, palette choice, noise offset) for uniqueness.
- **Normalization is an honesty requirement, not just an engineering detail.** Without NFC normalization, "Wałęsa" typed on one device and pasted from a PDF on another could produce two different signatures for the same canonical surname. The seed should be computed from the *canonical* surname resolved by the product, never from raw keyboard input. A diacritic-less typed form such as "Walesa" must first be resolved to its canonical form (or disambiguated by the user).
- **Polish diacritics as first-class geometry.** The nine Polish diacritic letters (ą ć ę ł ń ó ś ź ż; standard Polish orthography, not separately sourced here) fall into four mark families: kreska (acute), ogonek, kropka (overdot) and the stroke of ł. Each can get a distinct graphic mark. That makes "diacritics are identity" *visible* and gives letter-derived variety without inventing meaning. The digraphs ch, cz, dz, dź, dż, rz and sz can be treated as single units.
- **Avoid NFT-style "rare trait" labels** on the signature. Users would conflate "rare signature feature" with "rare surname" or "special family", which is a false factual implication.
- **Versioning keeps promises.** Art Blocks' guarantee is "same hash → same output". Name Atlas's equivalent: the algorithm version is part of the signature's identity (for example v1), so shared links keep rendering the same image even after the algorithm evolves.
- **Name-to-sound is an honest, centuries-old precedent.** An optional audio rendering of the surname's letters via a declared cryptogram key would be art derived from letters, not a claim.

### Gaps
- Dmitri Cherniak's *Ringers*, Zancan, and Matt DesLauriers's own essays were not fetched, so there are no verified specifics beyond the DesLauriers palette credit.
- "Name as sigil" projects were not researched.
- No empirical study was found testing whether letter-derived generative art is perceived as more personal than hash-random art. The name-letter effect concerns preference for letters, not artworks.
- Art Blocks details come only from a docs search summary.
- The mirror symmetry of GitHub identicons is not stated in the fetched post.

## 3. Shareable personal-data artifacts: what formats and content drive sharing, and what quantitative evidence exists?

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced share drivers are:
- comparative standing (percentiles, "top X%", "you vs others");
- an identity statement or archetype that gives a *second* reason to share;
- the vertical 9:16 "stories" format;
- self-disclosure itself, which is intrinsically rewarding.

Scale evidence: Spotify Wrapped 2025 reported 200M+ engaged users in 24 hours and 500M+ shares. These are company figures that count screenshots and downloads. The ancestry-card lesson: share cards that present *estimates* as precise identity facts erode trust when the model or data changes.

### Cited Findings
#### Numbers at a glance
- **Spotify Wrapped 2025** — [V] [TechCrunch, 4 Dec 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/12/04/spotify-says-wrapped-2025-is-its-biggest-yet-with-200m-users-in-its-first-day); [S] [Music Week](https://www.musicweek.com/digital/read/spotify-wrapped-2025-was-biggest-ever-with-200-million-engaged-users/093178)
  - More than 200M engaged users within 24 hours. "Engaged users are counted as those who have viewed at least one of the stories within the Wrapped experience."
  - Wrapped 2024 took 62 hours to reach 200M. Engagement was up 19% year over year.

  - AI was used behind the scenes, with no consumer-facing AI experience, unlike 2024's criticised AI podcast.

  - History: a 2019 newsletter, moved in-app in 2020.

  - The 2014 story videos: over 120,000 created in 24 hours. — [S] [Stink Studios](https://stinkstudios.com/work/your-2014-story)

#### Why people share (mechanisms)
- **Tamir & Mitchell (2012, PNAS)** — [V] [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3361411/)
  - "Humans devote 30–40% of speech output solely to informing others of their own subjective experiences."
  - Participants gave up money to talk about themselves: "an average loss of 17% of potential earnings" (Study 2) and about "25% of potential earnings on self shared trials" (Study 4).
  - Reward-region activity was greater for *shared* than for *private* self-disclosure.
- **Berger & Milkman (2012, JMR)**, on three months of NYT articles:
  - Positive content was more viral than negative.
  - High-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) increased virality; low-arousal sadness decreased it.
  - Surprising, interesting and practically useful content was also more viral.
  - [S] [Berger & Milkman PDF (Wharton)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf)

#### Trust problems of identity-data share cards
- **AncestryDNA ethnicity update (2018–2019)**
  - Ancestry's update used "5x as many samples", and some customers' results changed drastically. One example: from "57% Scottish and Irish and 10% England" to "62% British and 28% Scottish/Irish/Wales". Many users were upset.
  - A spokesperson said results are estimates that "will change as more data comes in".
  - [S] [ClickOrlando/WKMG](https://www.clickorlando.com/news/2019/04/30/ancestrycom-under-fire-after-update-changes-the-ethnicity-of-users); [S] [DNAeXplained](https://dna-explained.com/2019/11/20/making-sense-of-ethnicity-updates/); [S] [CBC](https://amp.cbc.ca/news/canada/nova-scotia/ancestry-dna-update-changes-explained-1.4836784)
- **23andMe**
  - A 2023 breach exposed data of about 6.9M customers, including ancestry reports, DNA matches and family names.
  - The company filed for Chapter 11 in March 2025. Afterwards 1.9M people (about 15% of customers) requested deletion of their genetic data.
  - A $30M class settlement followed, and in July 2026 42 attorneys general reached a settlement with the bankruptcy trustee.
  - [S] [Yahoo/AP](https://www.yahoo.com/news/genetic-testing-company-23andme-files-145618325.html); [S] [Georgia Consumer Protection](https://consumer.georgia.gov/node/5046)

### Inferences
- **Comparative framing is the strongest evidenced share lever.** Duolingo raised share rates by putting the percentile on the card. For Name Atlas, rarity *is* a comparative statement ("carried by N people in Poland, as of <date>"), so the product's core fact is inherently shareable.
- **Caveat on comparative framing.** Duolingo's shares were dominated by top performers (top 10% → >50% of shares). Surname rarity is not an achievement. If rare = "winner", holders of common surnames get a lesser card. Every surname needs an equally dignified card, for example "one of Poland's most-carried surnames" as a *fact*, not a status.
- **A second share reason matters.** Duolingo's archetype card "significantly boosted" shares. Name Atlas's honest equivalents of a personality type:
  - the **letter signature** (art, not fact);
  - the **diacritic identity**, e.g. "Ł and ę: two letters most keyboards outside Poland drop";
  - a **compare** card (two surnames side by side).

  Any archetype-like copy must be factual. Barnum-style personality statements would violate the "never invent a story" rule.
- **Screenshots are a primary share channel.** Spotify counts them as shares. Therefore every chapter screen should be *share-complete*: surname, fact, source and dataset date should all be visible on screen, not only on the exported card.
- **9:16 is the native container.** Wrapped's move to a stories format (2019) coincided with its social explosion (1.2M+ tweets that December). Correlation, not proven causation.
- **Social and multiplayer comparison is growing** (2025 Wrapped Party and listening-age comparisons). A user-initiated "compare two surnames" (e.g., a couple's or parents' surnames) is a natural social object. It must stay user-initiated, because it may reveal maiden names.
- **The ancestry lesson maps directly onto registry counts.** Counts will change with each registry release. Cards and pages must print "as of <dataset date>" so that an old shared card stays *true for its date* instead of becoming "wrong". Estimates must never be presented with more precision than the data supports.
- **The 23andMe lesson.** Identity-adjacent products live or die on trust. Do not require accounts to see results, collect minimal data, and put no personal data on cards beyond the surname the user chooses to share.
- **Wrapped's criticism ("free advertising", "exploitation of user labour")** implies that users notice when shares are primarily marketing. Keep branding on cards minimal and make the content the point.

### Gaps
- Apple Music Replay, Letterboxd Year in Review and BuzzFeed-style "What's your X" quizzes were not researched; no verified data.
- spotify.design was inaccessible. Articles such as "Making Moves: Designing Motion for 2022 Wrapped" now 301-redirect to open.spotify.com and return 404 (checked 2026-09-30), so there are no first-party design-process details.
- No public data found comparing share rates by aspect ratio (9:16 vs 1:1 vs link preview).

## 4. Narrative structures for data stories: which structure fits a 60–120 s mobile discovery of one's surname?

### Takeaway
The best fit is a **martini-glass structure with a drill-down bowl**, opened by a "stimulating default view". That means:
1. The answer comes first, within 10 seconds.
2. A short author-driven stem of about 5 chapters follows, advanced by native vertical scroll with scroll-triggered animated transitions.
3. The story then opens into reader-driven drill-downs.

Evidence behind the recommendation:
- NN/g data says the first 10 s decide whether people stay.
- The NYT found readers "just want to scroll".
- McKenna et al. found animated transitions raise engagement, and found no significant stepper-vs-scroller difference.
- Wrapped shows a tap-through stepper can work at massive scale for "about-you" content.

### Cited Findings
**Segel & Heer, "Narrative Visualization: Telling Stories with Data" (IEEE InfoVis 2010)** — [V] [PDF](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2010-Narrative-InfoVis.pdf) ([landing page](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/narrative))
- The design space was built from 58 examples.
- Seven genres: "magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, and film/video/animation".
- Author-driven vs reader-driven:
  - "A purely author-driven approach has a strict linear path through the visualization, relies heavily on messaging, and includes no interactivity... A strongly author-driven approach works best when the goal is storytelling or efficient communication."
  - A reader-driven approach "supports tasks such as data diagnostics, pattern discovery, and hypothesis formation."
  - "All the interactive examples in our dataset use a mix of the author-driven and reader-driven approaches."
- **Martini Glass:** "begins with an author-driven approach... Once the author's intended narrative is complete, the visualization opens up to a reader-driven stage where the user is free to interactively explore the data."
- **Interactive Slideshow:** "incorporates interaction mid-narrative within the confines of each slide"; "individual slides often function in the martini glass style".
- **Drill-Down Story:** "presents a general theme and then allows the user to choose among particular instances of that theme to reveal additional details and backstories."
- Under-used devices the authors highlight:
  - "Tacit tutorials introduce a visualization's interactive functionality by animating the interactive components along with the presentation."
  - "Stimulating default views provide initial presentations of data and analysis intended to excite the user, a device analogous to journalistic leads."
  - Narrative messaging such as "repetition of key points, introductory texts, and final summaries".

**McKenna, Henry Riche, Lee, Boy & Meyer, "Visual Narrative Flow" (EuroVis 2017)** — [V] [PDF](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~miriah/publications/narrative-flow.pdf)
- They analysed 80 stories and defined seven "flow-factors": navigation input, level of control, navigation progress, story layout, role of visualization, story progression and navigation feedback.
- Exploratory studies on stepper vs scroller preference:
  - "Three participants preferred the stepper while five preferred the scroller."
  - In a second study "only two (out of 10) readers preferred the stepper; the remaining eight preferring the scroller version".
  - Tablet users "recommended modifying the stepper for touch and to utilize swiping between steps."
- Crowdsourced study, n = 240:
  - "The animated transition conditions scored significantly higher on engagement than static visualizations."
  - "While steppers scored higher for engagement on average, the difference over scrollers was not significant."
  - Level of control (discrete vs continuous) "may not" matter.
- On progress indicators, "only 2 out of 10 interviewees preferred knowing these steps of the story to help them manage their time and attention."

**Archie Tse (NYT Deputy Graphics Director), "Why We Are Doing Fewer Interactives", Malofiej 2016** — [V] [slides PDF](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/archietse/malofiej-2016/master/tse-malofiej-2016-slides.pdf) ([repo](https://github.com/archietse/malofiej-2016))
- On steppers, tabs and sliders: "readers weren't getting to all of the content"; "Readers just want to scroll."
- The three rules:
  1. "If you make the reader click or do anything other than scroll, something spectacular has to happen."
  2. "If you make a tooltip or rollover, assume no one will ever see it. If content is important for readers to see, don't hide it."
  3. "When deciding whether to make something interactive, remember that getting it to work on all platforms is expensive."
- Other guidance: "If animation or motion is needed, try to trigger it when user scrolls"; "We still do interactives, but the bar is now VERY high."
- Note: an "85% of visitors ignore tooltips" figure circulates in secondary sources ([S] [futureofcoding thread](https://linen.futureofcoding.org/t/22673047/i-recall-coming-across-some-opinions-against-interactivity-i)) but **does not appear in the slides**. Do not use it.

**The Pudding, "Responsive scrollytelling best practices" (Russell Samora, Apr 2017)** — [V] [pudding.cool](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/)
- Mobile-first "forces you to pare down your experience to the nuts and bolts".
- Keep scroll-driven transitions only when they are "truly meaningful, and not just something to make it pop". Otherwise stack the charts.
- Avoid `vh` units, because "Mobile browsers toggle the top and bottom navbars' position and sizes".
- Replace hover with fixed annotations, and keep mobile "short and sweet" with fewer steps.
- The article also cautions against click-steppers and swipe/tap interfaces that override native scrolling (fetch paraphrase).

**Attention budget (NN/g, Jakob Nielsen, 11 Sep 2011)** — [V] [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- "Users often leave Web pages in 10–20 seconds, but pages with a clear value proposition can hold people's attention for much longer."
- It draws on Liu et al. (Microsoft Research, 2010), who analysed 205,000+ pages: dwell time follows a Weibull distribution, and 99% of pages show "negative aging" (the chance of leaving falls after the first seconds).
- "To gain several minutes of user attention, you must clearly communicate your value proposition within 10 seconds."

**Personal-input precedents**
- The NYT dialect quiz is a well-known input → personal-map interaction: 25 questions, then a map of where you are likely from. — [S] [Peabody Awards](https://peabodyawards.com/award-profile/how-yall-youse-and-you-guys-talk-ny-times-dialect-quiz-2013/)
- Wrapped's stories format is a tap-through stepper operating at a scale of hundreds of millions. — [V] [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spotify_Wrapped); [V] [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/12/04/spotify-says-wrapped-2025-is-its-biggest-yet-with-200m-users-in-its-first-day)

### Inferences
- **Structure: martini glass + drill-down bowl.** Search and social arrivals need the value proposition inside 10 s (NN/g). The stem should therefore *start* with a stimulating default view: canonical surname with diacritics, count, dataset date and confidence. That works like a journalistic lead.
- **The stem** is author-driven, about 5 chapters: rarity → letter signature → meaning (if evidenced) → one historical dimension → sources and share.
- **The bowl** is reader-driven drill-down: variants, regional distribution, full sources and method, related surnames, and (only when the evidence exists) the premium inventory.
- **Navigation.** Use native vertical scroll with scroll-triggered, animated chapter transitions. This follows Tse and The Pudding, and McKenna shows animation raises engagement. Evidence on steppers is mixed: Tse and The Pudding caution against them in journalism, McKenna is inconclusive, and Wrapped shows they work for self-focused content. Recommendation: scroll for the page, plus an optional tap-through "story mode" only for previewing and exporting the 9:16 share cards.
- **Each chapter should be standalone and screenshot-complete**, carrying its own source and date line. That serves both scroll readers and the screenshot share path.
- **Tacit tutorial.** Let the signature *draw itself from the letters while the user types*. This teaches the mapping without instructions (Segel & Heer's under-used device) and is the "something spectacular" that justifies the one required interaction: typing.
- **Progress indication should be subtle** (chapter ticks, not a numbered progress bar). Only 2 of 10 interviewees wanted explicit step counts, but orientation still helps on mobile.
- **End with a final summary**, which Segel & Heer found under-used. The summary card doubles as the default share card.

### Gaps
- No study was found on 60–120 s personal-data or name discovery on mobile specifically. The timing budget is an inference from NN/g dwell-time data.
- neal.fun (Neal Agarwal), Bret Victor's "Explorable Explanations" and Nicky Case's work were not fetched; no verified facts are included here.
- Wrapped's per-story completion or drop-off rates were not found.
- McKenna et al.'s stepper/scroller evidence came from desktop/tablet and crowdsourced settings; no phone-specific data.

## 5. Ethical engagement: curiosity, earned reveals, ritual, sound/haptics, calm design, and an honest paid offer

### Takeaway
Curiosity, visible effort and participation all measurably increase engagement and perceived value. Each becomes manipulation once decoupled from truth, for example by:
- teasing gaps that will not be closed;
- showing fake "work";
- turning a ritual into a toll;
- manufacturing urgency or scarcity around a purchase.

The regulatory context is tightening:
- An EU sweep found dark patterns on 148 of 399 shops (2023).
- A global ICPEN/GPEN sweep, in which UOKiK participated, found 75.7% of 642 sites and apps used at least one (2024).
- The DSA bans deceptive interfaces on online platforms, and the UCPD covers the rest.
- A Digital Fairness Act targeting dark patterns and addictive design is expected by end-2026.

Motion must be optional (WCAG 2.3.3), sound needs a user gesture (browser autoplay policy), and web haptics are unavailable on iPhone Safari.

### Cited Findings
**Curiosity and progressive disclosure**
- Loewenstein (1994), *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1): curiosity arises "when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge. Such information gaps produce the feeling of deprivation labeled curiosity. The curious individual is motivated to obtain the missing information to reduce or eliminate the feeling of deprivation." — [S] [bibliographic entry](https://stafforini.com/works/loewenstein-1994-psychology-curiosity-review/)
- Progressive disclosure (Jakob Nielsen, 3 Dec 2006) — [V] [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)
  - "Initially, show users only a few of the most important options. Offer a larger set of specialized options upon request."
  - It improves learnability, efficiency and error rate.
  - Designs should not exceed two levels, and progression needs strong "information scent".

**Visible effort and participation**
- Buell & Norton, "The Labor Illusion" (*Management Science* 57(9), 2011) — [V] [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v57y2011i9p1564-1579.html)
  - "When websites engage in operational transparency by signaling that they are exerting effort, people can actually prefer websites with longer waits to those that return instantaneous results--even when those results are identical."
  - Five experiments (online travel and dating). The effect is mediated by perceived effort and reciprocity, and the paper explores boundary conditions.
  - Secondary summaries add that "60%" preferred the longer transparent wait and that perceived value was "8% higher". — [S] [Agile Brand Guide](https://agilebrandguide.com/wiki/terms/labor-illusion/); not verified against the paper.
- IKEA effect (Norton, Mochon & Ariely, *J. Consumer Psychology*, 2012) — [S] [Wikipedia: IKEA effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IKEA_effect); [S] [paper PDF (Harvard DASH)](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/12136084/norton%20mochon%20ariely%20third%20round[1].pdf)
  - Labour increases people's valuation of what they made. A study reported willingness to pay 63% more for self-assembled furniture.
  - The effect dissipated when participants failed to complete, or built and then destroyed, their creations.

**Deceptive patterns: taxonomy and prevalence**
- deceptive.design (the resource builds on Harry Brignull's "dark patterns" work) lists 18 types — [V] [deceptive.design/types](https://www.deceptive.design/types)
  - The full list: Sneaking, Forced action, Hard to cancel, Preselection, Obstruction, Hidden subscription, Hidden costs, Trick wording, Visual interference, Fake social proof, Fake urgency, Nagging, Fake scarcity, Disguised ads, Confirmshaming, Comparison prevention, Addictive design, Currency confusion.
  - Definitions most relevant to a premium offer (verbatim):
    - **Sneaking:** "The user is drawn into a transaction on false pretences, because pertinent information is hidden or delayed from being presented to them."
    - **Hidden costs:** "The user is enticed with a low advertised price. After investing time and effort, they discover unexpected fees and charges when they reach the checkout."
    - **Fake urgency:** "The user is pressured into completing an action because they are presented with a fake time limitation."
    - **Fake scarcity:** "...a fake indication of limited supply or popularity."
    - **Confirmshaming:** "The user is emotionally manipulated into doing something that they would not otherwise have done."
    - **Nagging:** "...persistently interrupted by requests to do something else that may not be in their best interests."
    - **Addictive design:** "...exploits psychological vulnerabilities to foster compulsive behaviour."
- Mathur et al., "Dark Patterns at Scale" (CSCW 2019) — [S] [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.07032)
  - About 53K product pages on about 11K shopping sites were crawled.
  - They found 1,818 dark-pattern instances (15 types, 7 categories) on 1,254 sites (≈11.1%).
  - 183 sites used outright deceptive practices, and 22 third parties sold dark patterns as turnkey solutions. More popular sites were more likely to use them.
- **European Commission and CPC Network sweep** (press release IP/23/418, 30 Jan 2023) — [V] [EC press release PDF](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/api/files/document/print/en/ip_23_418/IP_23_418_EN.pdf)
  - 399 online shops were screened; "148 sites contained at least one of these three dark patterns".
  - 42 used fake countdown timers.
  - 54 directed consumers "either through their visual design or choice of language".
  - 70 hid important information, 23 of them to push a subscription.
  - Apps of 102 of the sites were also checked; 27 used at least one of the patterns.
  - Commissioner Reynders: "nearly 40% of the online shopping websites rely on manipulative practices to exploit consumers' vulnerabilities or trick them."
  - The DSA "will prohibit dark patterns on online platforms", complementing the UCPD and GDPR.
- **ICPEN/GPEN global sweep** (29 Jan–2 Feb 2024) — [S] [ICPEN](https://icpen.org/news/1360); [S] [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/07/ftc-icpen-gpen-announce-results-review-use-dark-patterns-affecting-subscription-services-privacy)
  - 27 authorities from 26 countries took part.
  - Of 642 websites and apps, 75.7% used at least one dark pattern and 66.8% used two or more.
  - Sneaking (e.g., auto-renewal that cannot be turned off) and interface interference were frequent.
- **Poland / UOKiK** — [S] [wiadomoscikosmetyczne.pl](https://www.wiadomoscikosmetyczne.pl/rynek-kosmetyczny/uokik-wzial-udzial-w-globalnym-przegladzie-internetu-pod-katem-dark-patterns-2514434); [S] [Bird & Bird, 2023](https://twobirds.com/en/insights/2023/poland/dark-patterns-becoming-a-new-hot-topic-for-the-polish-regulator); [S] [PAP](https://www.pap.pl/aktualnosci/uokik-w-trakcie-black-friday-trzeba-uwazac-min-na-zwodnicze-interfejsy-wideo); [S] [xyz.pl](https://xyz.pl/?p=180391)
  - UOKiK took part in the ICPEN sweep.
  - Its most frequently detected patterns were preselection and "most popular" labels (social proof).

  - Bird & Bird (2023) called dark patterns "a new hot topic for the Polish regulator".

**EU law**

**Calm technology**

### Inferences
- **Curiosity gaps are ethical when three conditions hold:**
  - (a) the gap is real (there *is* content behind it);
  - (b) it is closed on the same page, for free, within the session;
  - (c) no open loop is left hanging over a paywall.

  "Absence of evidence" can itself close a gap honestly: "We found no reliable evidence about the meaning of this surname in <sources> as of <date>."
- **Operational transparency is legitimate only when it mirrors real operations** (the labor illusion). Examples: "Resolving diacritics → canonical form", "Reading registry dataset (2024-xx-xx)", "Checking historical sources". Artificial delays or fake step lists would be deception. The same "effort" UI that raises perceived value becomes a misleading practice if the work is not happening.
- **Rituals work only if completable and meaningful.** The IKEA effect vanishes on failure, and Tse sets the bar at "something spectacular". So:
  - Typing the surname is the one required ritual.
  - Tracing, holding or dragging should be optional flourishes with single-tap alternatives. WCAG 2.5.1 (Pointer Gestures) and 2.5.7 (Dragging Movements) apply; they were not fetched in this pass, so verify them.
  - A ritual must never gate facts.
- **Sound and haptics are progressive enhancements.**
  - Sound needs an explicit gesture (autoplay policy), should be opt-in, and the experience must be complete in silence.
  - Haptics cannot be relied on for iPhone users, because iOS Safari lacks the Vibration API. The Polish iOS share was not researched.
- **The legal route likely runs through the UCPD, not DSA Art. 25.** Art. 25 applies to *online platforms* (services disseminating user content to the public) and explicitly defers to the UCPD for practices it covers. A surname publisher selling a report is squarely a B2C commercial practice under the UCPD (and Polish implementing law, enforced by UOKiK). This is not legal advice; it needs review. Designing to Art. 25(3)'s examples and the DFA's announced targets anyway future-proofs the product.
- **Duolingo's "badge for sharing" and Wrapped's "free advertising" critique** show where incentive design drifts toward extraction. Name Atlas should not reward, gate or nag for shares.
- **Calm technology's "work even when it fails"** maps directly onto the invariant "absence is not zero". Missing data is a designed, dignified state, not an error or an upsell.

### Gaps
- The EU UCPD Guidance (2021) section on data-driven practices and dark patterns, and the Polish implementing act, were not fetched.
- Brignull's book *Deceptive Patterns* (2023) and the FTC's 2022 staff report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" were not consulted.
- No empirical research was found on "honest inventory" versus teaser paywalls, or on conversion/trust effects of transparent premium offers.
- Slow technology (Hallnäs & Redström 2001) was not fetched.
- No evidence was found on the effect of sound or haptics on engagement for web data stories.
- The labor-illusion "60%" and "8%" figures, and the IKEA "63%" figure, are from secondary summaries.

## 6. Implications for Name Atlas (synthesis across key questions)

### Takeaway
Build Name Atlas around a strict separation:
- **the signature is art made from the letters;**
- **the data layer shows validated claims with source, date and confidence.**

Put the answer first. Tell a five-chapter martini-glass story by native scroll with meaningful motion. Make every screen share-complete in 9:16. Treat engagement mechanics (curiosity, ritual, effort display, premium) as legitimate only when each is backed by something true and closed in-session.

### Inferences

#### A. An honest "name signature" generative system

**Principle: "a portrait of the letters, not of the family."**

**Allowed inputs** are string-derived only, and anyone can verify them by looking at the name:
1. The **canonical surname** resolved by the product, never the raw keystrokes. It is Unicode-NFC-normalized; Polish-locale lower-casing is used for seeding, while display casing is preserved.
2. **Letter units in order.** Polish digraphs (ch, cz, dz, dź, dż, rz, sz) may count as single units under a declared, versioned rule.
3. **Diacritics by type and position:**
   - kreska: ć ń ó ś ź
   - ogonek: ą ę
   - kropka: ż
   - stroke: ł

   Each type is a distinct, consistent mark. This is where "diacritics are identity" becomes visible.
4. **Length, vowel/consonant rhythm, repeated letters and symmetry**, and **syllable count** (from a deterministic, versioned orthographic rule; label it "orthographic syllables").
5. **Structural features:** a hyphen in a double-barrelled surname becomes a two-part composition. Suffix *shapes* (e.g., "-ski/-ska") are allowed as pure string forms. Gendered pairs (Kowalski/Kowalska) can share a visible stem with a different ending mark, but the suffix must **never** be given semantic meaning such as "noble".
6. **A seed** = hash(algorithm-version + normalized canonical string), fed into a seeded PRNG for the ornamental layer only (grain, noise offset, choice among curated palettes). Never use `Math.random()` or time.

**Disallowed inputs** (they would turn art into an implied claim):
- meaning or etymology;
- origin region, ethnicity or nationality;
- heraldry/coat of arms or "noble" status;
- historical events and any facts about ancestors;
- anything with confidence below "validated";
- **also** the registry count and rarity.

Verified data changes with each dataset release. If data drove the art, the "signature" would mutate, as the Ancestry estimates did. Show data in a separate, labelled data layer (for example a count glyph or dot field with the source and date printed) *around* the signature, never inside it.

**Rules:**
- **Legible:** a "How this was drawn" legend. Tap a stroke to highlight its letter, and state: "Drawn only from the letters of <Surname>. It says nothing about your family's history."
- **Two layers:** a transparent structural layer (related names look related) plus a hash-seeded ornamental layer (uniqueness).
- **Immutable per version:** same canonical + version → identical pixels on client, server (OG image) and export. Put the version in shareable URLs.
- **Long-form QA:** before launch, render *every* canonical surname in the registry and screen automatically for degenerate outputs (too sparse or dense, clipping, low contrast), plus human review of samples. Include extreme cases: very short names, very long names, names dense with diacritics, hyphenated names.
- **No heraldic vocabulary:** no shields, crowns, eagles, crests, or "old-document" styling that implies antiquity. No "rare trait" labels on signature features.
- **Brand as the constant:** the Name Atlas frame, grid and typeface stay fixed, like the MIT grid or OCAD's windows. Only the signature varies inside it.
- **Optional honest sound:** a musical-cryptogram rendering of the letters with a declared key, opt-in after a gesture, silent by default.

#### B. Share-card formats

**Formats**
- **9:16 story card** (primary): signature + canonical surname (correct diacritics) + one validated fact + "Source: <dataset>, as of <date>" + short URL.
- **1:1 card** (feed, messaging).
- **~1.91:1 link preview**: a dynamic Open Graph image rendered server-side from the same deterministic signature. Pixel specs are unverified; check them against current Meta documentation.

**Card taxonomy (all opt-in, all showing source/date/confidence where factual)**

| Card | Content | Conditions and guardrails |
|---|---|---|
| **Rarity** (the social object) | "<N> people in Poland carry the surname <X>." A "1 in N residents" phrasing only if a population denominator with the same reference date exists. | No status language ("elite", "top 1%"). Common surnames get an equally proud card ("one of Poland's most-carried surnames", if ranked from data). |
| **Letters** (signature) | The art + "drawn from the letters: Ł, ę…" | Always carries the legend line. |
| **Diacritics** | "Your surname has 2 Polish diacritics: ł, ę" | A string fact; always true; very diaspora-relevant. |
| **Meaning** | The meaning statement with its confidence label. | Only when confidence meets the validated threshold. Never shown for weak evidence. |
| **Compare** | Two surnames side by side (signatures + counts). | User-initiated only (privacy of maiden names). |
| **Summary** | The final chapter recap. | The default export. |

**Every card**
- carries no user personal data beyond the chosen surname, no ancestor claims and no heraldry;
- has minimal branding;
- has alt text;
- previews exactly what will be posted;
- uses a typeface with full Polish (Latin Extended-A) coverage in the renderer.

**Screenshot-completeness:** every on-page chapter carries its own source/date line, because screenshots are a major share path.

#### C. Recommended 60–120 s discovery narrative (martini glass + drill-down)

| Time | Chapter | Content and mechanics |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 s | **Input ritual** | The user types the surname. Letter strokes begin forming as they type (tacit tutorial). |
| 5–10 s | **Resolve** | Diacritic suggestion or disambiguation if needed (e.g., "Walesa → Wałęsa?"). Real operational steps shown only if they are real. |
| by ~10 s | **Answer (stimulating default view)** | Canonical surname, count, dataset date and confidence chip. This fully satisfies search intent with no scroll required. |
| 10–25 s | **Ch. 1 Rarity** | A rarity statement with an animated unit visual (scroll-triggered). |
| 25–40 s | **Ch. 2 Your letters** | The signature completes; legend; optional trace/hold flourish with a tap alternative. |
| 40–70 s | **Ch. 3 Meaning / origin** | Evidence-backed only. Confidence labels. An explicit "what we don't know" line. |
| 70–95 s | **Ch. 4 One historical dimension** | For example an earliest attestation or historical distribution, with its source. |
| 95–120 s | **Ch. 5 Sources & share** | A full source list; the summary card; share and compare. |
| open-ended | **Bowl** (reader-driven drill-down) | Variants, regional map, method and data notes, related surnames. **Only if genuine deeper evidence exists:** the premium inventory card. |

**Mechanics**
- Native vertical scroll with gentle snap; no scroll-jacking.
- Scroll-triggered animated transitions, with a full static equivalent under `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- No content in hovers or tooltips.
- Subtle chapter ticks, no countdowns.
- An optional tap-through "story mode" only for previewing and exporting cards.
- A clear end state: "That's the story so far."

#### D. Ethical engagement rules

1. **Answer first, ungated.** Canonical form, count, date and confidence appear in the first view. They are never behind an interaction, sign-up, share or payment.
2. **Close every curiosity gap you open, free, in-session.** No teaser for content that does not exist or is paid. No "you won't believe…" copy.
3. **Rituals are invitations, not tolls.** Typing is the only required act. Trace, hold and drag are optional, skippable, have single-tap alternatives, are completable by everyone, and always produce something real.
4. **Show real work only.** Progress and "checking sources" states must mirror real operations and real durations. Never add artificial delay (the labor illusion is only honest as true operational transparency).
5. **No manufactured urgency, scarcity or social proof.** No countdowns, "today only", "N people are viewing this surname" or "limited reports". The EU sweep found fake timers on 42 of 399 shops; do not be one.
6. **Premium = honest inventory, shown only when the evidence exists.**
   - State exactly what the report contains: record types, counts, date ranges, sources.
   - State what it does not contain: no ancestors, no coat of arms.
   - Show the full price including VAT up front, and say whether it is one-time or a subscription.
   - No blurred or fake preview content, no pre-selected extras.
   - Cancellation and refund must be as easy as purchase.
7. **Ask once and remember "no".** The premium mention appears in a fixed place, at most once per session. Dismissal persists. No confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't care about my roots") and no nagging (DSA Art. 25(3) examples).
8. **Label art as art and facts as facts.**
   - Signature: "drawn from the letters".
   - Data: source + date + confidence.
   - The signature never changes when data changes.
9. **Show absence as absence.** "No reliable evidence found in <sources> as of <date>". Never "0", and never spin "unknown" into "rare" or "mysterious".
10. **Rarity without status.** State rarity as a count or ratio with its date. No rank-as-merit language. Common surnames get equally dignified treatment.
11. **Motion is expressive but optional.** Honour `prefers-reduced-motion` with an equivalent static path (WCAG 2.3.3). Auto-moving content longer than 5 s needs pause/stop (WCAG 2.2.2).
12. **Sound is opt-in after a gesture; haptics are enhancement only.** The experience is complete in silence and without vibration (iOS Safari lacks the Vibration API).
13. **Share without coercion.** No share-to-unlock, no rewards or badges for sharing, and a preview of exactly what will be posted. No tracking identifiers tied to a person on share links.
14. **Finite by design.** The stem ends with a clear summary. No infinite feed, streaks, re-engagement notifications or "come back tomorrow" hooks (calm technology; the DFA's "addictive design" target).
15. **No claims about the user.** Copy says "people with this surname" and "the surname", never "your ancestors were…" or "your family came from…". No heraldic imagery anywhere.
16. **Privacy by default.** Results without an account, surname lookups not linked to identity, and aggregate-only analytics. If a purchase requires data, collect the minimum and keep it separate from lookups (the 23andMe trust lesson).
17. **Measure honestly.** Define engagement and share metrics the way you would disclose them publicly (Spotify, for example, defines "engaged" as viewing one story). Don't optimise for time-on-page beyond the 60–120 s story.

### Gaps
- None of these recommendations has been tested with Name Atlas's audience. Card types, narrative pacing and the premium inventory card need usability and A/B validation, including a check of share rates for rare versus common surnames.
- A legal review of the premium offer under the UCPD and Polish implementing law is needed. Whether any part of the product is an "online platform" under the DSA (e.g., if it hosts user content) was not assessed.
- WCAG 2.5.1 and 2.5.7 (gesture and dragging alternatives) and exact platform image specs were not fetched.
- The availability of a same-date population denominator for "1 in N" statements was not researched here.
- The Polish iOS vs Android share, which matters for haptics, and the Polish social-platform mix were not researched.
