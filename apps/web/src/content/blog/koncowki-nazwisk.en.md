Many people try to guess where a Polish surname comes from by its ending. Kowalski sounds noble, Mickiewicz sounds like the old eastern borderlands, Kowalczyk sounds homely. Some of these impressions have a basis in history, but an ending mostly tells you how the surname was formed. It does not tell you where your family came from.

## -ski, -cki, -dzki: from a place

The suffix -ski and its variants -cki and -dzki originally marked a place or ownership. The oldest of these surnames said where someone came from: Tarnowski from Tarnów, Brzeziński from Brzezie. The nobility, who took their names from their estates, carried them, which is why -ski surnames came to be seen as noble. Around the turn of the sixteenth century townspeople and peasants began to adopt them. The suffix then lost its original meaning and became the most productive ending in Polish surnames, neutral in sense. Our [first post](/en/blog/genealogia) has more on the history of -ski surnames.

## -wicz, -owicz, -ewicz: son of

Surnames ending in -icz, -owicz and -ewicz are patronymic: they were formed from a father's first name, byname, nickname or occupation. Michał Mordań of the University of Białystok gives examples from the Podlasie region: Jakubowicz from Jakub, Janowicz from Jan, Józefowicz from Józef, Kasperowicz from Kasper. The earliest names of this type are attested in the twelfth century.

These suffixes are East Slavic variants of a Proto-Slavic suffix. They were typical of Ruthenian naming and of the Polish and East Slavic borderlands. In the Grand Duchy of Lithuania they were used by boyars, townspeople and even peasants. From the seventeenth century they appeared more and more often in Polish surnames too, especially among townspeople.

A common belief holds that -owicz surnames belong to townspeople. The research Mordań cites questions this: in Polish towns from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century, -ski surnames prevailed, and the stereotype of the town-dwelling -owicz probably took shape only in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

## -ak, -ek, -ik, -czyk: son, diminutive or place

These suffixes did several jobs at once. They formed surnames from a father's name, like Adamiak or Staszczyk. They formed them from an occupation: the word kowal (blacksmith) gave Kowalczyk and Kowalik, alongside Kowal, Kowalski and Kowalewski. The suffix -czyk also formed surnames from place names, like Lubelczyk from Lublin. According to Mordań, the suffixes -ik, -czyk, -ek, -ka and -ko were also used to make diminutive forms of first names. So the ending -ak or -czyk alone is not enough to tell what a surname means.

## -uk: the eastern borderlands

The suffix -uk is characteristic of surnames in the Polish and East Slavic borderlands, and it formed patronymic surnames. Michał Sajewicz wrote a long monograph on -uk surnames in Hajnówka county in the Białystok region, comparing them with the rest of Poland. In the material he studied, most of these surnames were based on first names, often in shortened form and often from the Orthodox calendar.

## -owa and -ówna: women in old records

Today the difference between a woman's and a man's surname shows mainly in adjectival surnames: Kowalski and Kowalska. In the past the ending also told you whether a woman was married. A wife or widow used her husband's surname with -owa or -ina: Nowak and Nowakowa, Koba and Kobina. An unmarried daughter used her father's surname with -ówna or -anka: Kordziak and Kordziakówna, Morawa and Morawianka. The custom died out in the twentieth century.

That gives family historians a practical tip. When you look for a great-grandmother in old parish books, also check the forms with -owa and -ówna, because she may be recorded as someone's daughter or someone's wife.

## What an ending cannot tell you

An ending tells you how a surname was formed: from a place, from a father's name or from an occupation. It does not tell you where your ancestors lived or what social class they belonged to, because suffixes moved between social groups and regions. The same surname could also arise independently in many families.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a surname ending in -wicz Lithuanian or Belarusian?

Not necessarily. The suffixes -icz, -owicz and -ewicz are East Slavic in origin and were typical of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, but from the seventeenth century they spread in Polish surnames too. The ending alone does not settle where a family came from.

### What does a surname ending in -ak mean?

It often comes from a father's name, like Adamiak from Adam, but suffixes of this kind also formed surnames from occupations and diminutives of first names. The meaning of a particular surname is best checked in a dictionary such as Kazimierz Rymut's *Nazwiska Polaków*.

### Why is a woman called Kowalska and a man Kowalski?

Surnames ending in -ski, -cki and -dzki are adjectives, so they have a masculine and a feminine form: -ska, -cka and -dzka.

### Are the Kowalskis and the Kowalczyks one family?

The surname does not tell you that. Both come from the word kowal, but they were formed with different suffixes and could have arisen in completely unrelated families. Only records or DNA testing can confirm a relationship.

### How do I find a great-grandmother in old records?

Check several forms of the surname: the masculine, the feminine and the older forms with -owa and -ówna. Our [first post](/en/blog/genealogia) covers Geneteka's indexes and the scans on Szukajwarchiwach.
