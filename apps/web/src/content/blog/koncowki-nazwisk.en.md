Many people try to guess where a Polish surname comes from by its ending. Kowalski sounds noble, Mickiewicz sounds like the old eastern borderlands, Kowalczyk sounds homely. Some of these impressions have a basis in history, but an ending mostly tells you how the surname was formed. It does not tell you where your family came from.

## -ski, -cki, -dzki: what the -ski ending means

The suffix -ski forms adjectives, as in the words polski (Polish) and miejski (urban). In surnames it originally marked that someone came from a particular place or owned it. That is how the surname dictionary of the Institute of the Polish Language at the Polish Academy of Sciences (IJP PAN) describes its original function. According to Culture.pl, the oldest of these surnames began to spread around the thirteenth century and said where someone came from: Tarnowski from Tarnów, Brzeziński from Brzezie.

Zofia Kaleta shows from old records that a -ski surname meant the same as a phrase with the preposition z (from), in Latin de. In one document a nobleman is recorded as *nobilis Nicolaus Zubrski, heres de Zubrza*. The surname Zubrski and the words "heir de Zubrza" carry the same information.

### -cki and -dzki: the same suffix with a different sound

Whether the result is -ski, -cki or -dzki depends on the last sound of the word the suffix is added to. Krystyna Kowalik of IJP PAN describes this for adjectives formed from place names. After c, ć, k and t you get -cki: Łańcut and łańcucki, Biecz and biecki, Świątniki and świątnicki. After d, dz and dź you get -dzki: Tarnogród and tarnogrodzki, Sieradz and sieradzki. Kowalik treats -cki and -dzki as forms of the suffix -ski that depend on these neighbouring sounds. So a surname ending in -cki or -dzki means nothing different from one ending in -ski. It only sounds different.

### Why -ski came to sound noble

The nobility took their names mainly from the villages and towns they owned, hence the Zamojskis, the Koniecpolskis and the Tarnowskis. Ewa Wolnicz-Pawłowska notes that townspeople and peasants at the time mostly used names from their fathers' first names and from nicknames. Kaleta shows that a -ski surname was the one considered truly worthy of a nobleman, although surnames were formed in many other ways. In her account, the ending gained further prestige when the Lithuanian and Ruthenian nobility within the Polish state adopted the belief that -ski surnames had a special value.

### How -ski reached townspeople and peasants

By the sixteenth century the model was so clear that people remade their own surnames to fit it. Wolnicz-Pawłowska gives the example of a nobleman recorded in 1576 as Skowron who in the following years appears as Skowroński, and of Czajka, who changed his name to Czajkowski. There were very many such changes, especially among the petty nobility. According to Culture.pl, townspeople and peasants began to adopt -ski surnames around the turn of the sixteenth century. It gives examples of such changes: Ryba to Rybiński, Kaczmarek to Kaczmarski.

Wolnicz-Pawłowska also quotes writers of the time. Szymon Starowolski described a nobleman who dresses a peasant up as his servant and calls him Kijakowski or Wojtkowski. Walerian Nekanda-Trepka complained in *Liber chamorum* that peasants' sons took -ski surnames, most often from first names, such as Jakubowski or Piotrowski. The law set no penalty for this.

The IJP PAN dictionary says that among -ski surnames a significant share were formed from the sixteenth century onwards from ordinary words, on the model of surnames from place names, which were then seen as "better". The same surname could also arise twice, each time in a different way. The dictionary's example is Lenartowski, which may come from the village of Lenartowice or from the surname Lenart. The full history of Polish surnames, from the Middle Ages to PESEL, is in our [post on where Polish surnames came from](/en/blog/skad-wziely-sie-nazwiska-w-polsce).

### Why -ski is neutral today

According to Culture.pl, once townspeople and peasants adopted -ski surnames, the suffix lost its original sense of place or ownership and became the most productive ending in Polish surnames, neutral in sense. Wolnicz-Pawłowska observes that many modern -ski surnames have no link to a place name at all. Kaleta writes that the esteem for -ski surnames was still strong between the two world wars, and that after the Second World War they came to be valued the same as any other.

### Does -ski mean nobility?

No. Kowalski may sound noble, but the sources say otherwise. From the sixteenth century, people outside the nobility took -ski surnames. Many were formed from ordinary words or first names, not from the name of a village or an estate. The same surname could also arise in different families and in different ways. So the ending alone tells you neither that a family was noble nor that it has a right to a coat of arms. Only records show which social class your ancestors belonged to.

### -ska, -cka, -dzka

Because surnames ending in -ski, -cki and -dzki are adjectives, a woman uses the feminine form: Kowalska, Nowicka, Zawadzka. Why that is, and what happens to a surname on marriage, is in our [post on Kowalski and Kowalska](/en/blog/kowalski-czy-kowalska).

## -wicz, -owicz, -ewicz: son of

Surnames ending in -icz, -owicz and -ewicz are patronymic: they were formed from a father's first name, byname, nickname or occupation. Michał Mordań of the University of Białystok gives examples from the Podlasie region: Jakubowicz from Jakub, Janowicz from Jan, Józefowicz from Józef, Kasperowicz from Kasper. The earliest names of this type are attested in the twelfth century.

These suffixes are East Slavic variants of a Proto-Slavic suffix. They were typical of Ruthenian naming and of the Polish and East Slavic borderlands. In the Grand Duchy of Lithuania they were used by boyars, townspeople and even peasants. From the seventeenth century they appeared more and more often in Polish surnames too, especially among townspeople.

A common belief holds that -owicz surnames belong to townspeople. The research Mordań cites questions this: in Polish towns from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century, -ski surnames prevailed, and the stereotype of the town-dwelling -owicz probably took shape only in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

## -ak, -ek, -ik, -czyk: son, diminutive or place

These suffixes did several jobs at once. They formed surnames from a father's name, like Adamiak or Staszczyk. They formed them from an occupation: the word kowal (blacksmith) gave Kowalczyk and Kowalik, alongside Kowal, Kowalski and Kowalewski. The suffix -czyk also formed surnames from place names, like Lubelczyk from Lublin. According to Mordań, the suffixes -ik, -czyk, -ek, -ka and -ko were also used to make diminutive forms of first names. So the ending -ak or -czyk alone is not enough to tell what a surname means.

## -uk: the eastern borderlands

The suffix -uk is characteristic of surnames in the Polish and East Slavic borderlands, and it formed patronymic surnames. Michał Sajewicz wrote a long monograph on -uk surnames in Hajnówka county in the Białystok region, comparing them with the rest of Poland. In the material he studied, most of these surnames were based on first names, often in shortened form and often from the Orthodox calendar.

## -owa and -ówna: women in old records

Today the difference between a woman's and a man's surname shows mainly in adjectival surnames: Kowalski and Kowalska. In the past the ending also told you whether a woman was married. A wife or widow used her husband's surname with -owa or -ina: Nowak and Nowakowa, Koba and Kobina. An unmarried daughter used her father's surname with -ówna or -anka: Kordziak and Kordziakówna, Morawa and Morawianka. The custom died out in the twentieth century.

That gives family historians a practical tip. When you look for a great-grandmother in old parish books, also check the forms with -owa and -ówna, because she may be recorded as someone's daughter or someone's wife.

## What an ending cannot tell you

An ending tells you how a surname was formed: from a place, from a father's name or from an occupation. It does not tell you where your ancestors lived or what social class they belonged to, because suffixes moved between social groups and regions. The same surname could also arise independently in many families.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a surname ending in -wicz Lithuanian or Belarusian?

Not necessarily. The suffixes -icz, -owicz and -ewicz are East Slavic in origin and were typical of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, but from the seventeenth century they spread in Polish surnames too. The ending alone does not settle where a family came from.

### What does a surname ending in -ak mean?

It often comes from a father's name, like Adamiak from Adam, but suffixes of this kind also formed surnames from occupations and diminutives of first names. The meaning of a particular surname is best checked in a dictionary such as Kazimierz Rymut's *Nazwiska Polaków*.

### What does the ending -ski mean in a surname?

Originally, that someone came from a particular place or owned it: Tarnowski from Tarnów. From the sixteenth century -ski surnames were also formed from ordinary words and first names, on the model of surnames from places, so today the ending does not tell you where someone came from.

### How is -ski different from -cki and -dzki?

Only in sound. It is the same suffix: -cki appears after c, ć, k and t, and -dzki after d, dz and dź, as in the adjectives łańcucki and sieradzki.

### Does a surname ending in -ski mean noble descent?

No. From the sixteenth century townspeople and peasants also took -ski surnames, and many of them do not come from any village. Records, not the ending, confirm which social class your ancestors belonged to.

### Why is a woman called Kowalska and a man Kowalski?

Surnames ending in -ski, -cki and -dzki are adjectives, so they have a masculine and a feminine form: -ska, -cka and -dzka. There is more in our [post on Kowalski and Kowalska](/en/blog/kowalski-czy-kowalska).

### Are the Kowalskis and the Kowalczyks one family?

The surname does not tell you that. Both come from the word kowal, but they were formed with different suffixes and could have arisen in completely unrelated families. Only records or DNA testing can confirm a relationship.

### How do I find a great-grandmother in old records?

Check several forms of the surname: the masculine, the feminine and the older forms with -owa and -ówna. Our [first post](/en/blog/genealogia) covers Geneteka's indexes and the scans on Szukajwarchiwach.
