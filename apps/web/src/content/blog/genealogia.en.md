Sooner or later many people ask who their ancestors were and where their surname came from. Sometimes it starts with an old photo that nobody labelled. Sometimes it starts with a grandmother who remembers the name of a village but not the parish where her grandfather was baptised. That is where genealogy, the study of family history, begins.

## Why people look for their ancestors

The British sociologist Wendy Bottero studied how people research their family history. In a 2015 article she describes genealogy as a leisure hobby, a form of historical research and a task of processing information, all at once. In the family histories she collected, people described their research as a personal journey of discovery and came to identify with particular ancestors. Bottero also shows that this sense of identity grows out of the work itself: the records, dates and names that have to be found and pieced together.

For Polish families there is also emigration. Poland's Ministry of Foreign Affairs estimates that about 20 million Poles and people of Polish descent live abroad. For their descendants, a surname can be the first clue that leads back to Poland. Zofia Kaleta, who wrote a monograph on Polish surnames, gave a separate part of her book to how emigrant families relate to their names.

## Where surnames come from: a short history of Polish surnames

A surname was not always inherited. For a long time it was a byname added to a person's first name, and it did not have to pass to their children. Kaleta finds that bynames began to work as family names mainly in the fourteenth century, first among the emerging nobility and later among townspeople. A legal duty to carry a hereditary surname came to the Polish lands at the end of the eighteenth century, through the laws of Austria, Prussia and Russia after the partitions. Her reviewer, the linguist Ladislav Zgusta, adds that the Council of Trent, which sat from 1545 to 1563, had already sanctioned hereditary family names in Catholic countries.

Kaleta identifies eight ways Polish surnames were formed. Among them are surnames formed:

- from the father's name, like Adamiak,
- from a place name with the suffix -ski, like Zborowski,
- from a place name with another suffix, like Lubelczyk from Lublin,
- from an ordinary noun, like Korytko,
- from a variant of a first name, like Klesz from Klement,
- from a name taken from another language, like Steynbrecher.

Surnames ending in -ski were formed from place names, usually the place where the family held land. Over time they became a sign of belonging to the nobility, and they were so desirable that people with other surnames tried to adopt them. So the -ski ending on its own does not prove noble ancestry.

## Where to find Polish ancestors

Start at home, with documents, photos and conversations with your oldest relatives. First names, dates of birth and marriage, and the names of parishes are enough to move on to the archives. These sources are online:

- [Geneteka](https://geneteka.genealodzy.pl/) is the Polish Genealogical Society's database of indexes to parish registers. On 3 October 2026 it listed more than 68 million entries from more than 16,000 parishes.
- [Szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl](https://www.szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl/) is the portal of the Polish State Archives, run by the National Digital Archive. You can browse scans of parish books and civil registration records there for free.
- [Polona](https://polona.pl/) is the digital library of the National Library of Poland. You can search old books and periodicals in which a surname sometimes appears.
- *Nazwiska Polaków* by Kazimierz Rymut is a historical and etymological dictionary of Polish surnames, published by the Institute of the Polish Language of the Polish Academy of Sciences.
- [The list of surnames in the PESEL register](https://dane.gov.pl/pl/dataset/1681,nazwiska-osob-zyjacych-wystepujace-w-rejestrze-pesel) is published by Poland's Ministry of Digital Affairs. It gives the number of living people who bear each surname.

## What Name Atlas shows

Name Atlas starts with a surname. Type one in and you will see how many people bear it today according to the PESEL register, how rare it is, in which voivodeships it is most common, and how the number of its bearers has changed since 2019. Where a source gives them, you will also see its meaning and where it appears in old prints in Polona. Every figure has a source and a date.

One caveat: we talk about the surname, not about your family. Where most people with a surname live today does not tell you where your ancestors came from. For that you need the records. [Look up your surname](/?lang=en), free and without an account.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I start a family tree?

Start with yourself and your closest relatives. Write down what you know about your parents, grandparents and great-grandparents: names, dates, places and parishes. Then gather documents at home and talk to your oldest relatives. With that information it is easier to search Geneteka's indexes and the scans on Szukajwarchiwach.

### Does a surname ending in -ski mean noble ancestry?

No. Surnames ending in -ski were linked with the nobility, but people outside it adopted them too, precisely because they were seen as better. Ancestry has to be confirmed in documents.

### How can I find out what my surname means?

Look in dictionaries written by linguists, such as Kazimierz Rymut's *Nazwiska Polaków*. Name Atlas shows a surname's meaning only when a source gives it, and always names that source.

### Are people with the same surname related?

Not necessarily. Surnames were formed from first names, ordinary nouns and place names, so the same surname could arise in many unrelated families. Only records or DNA testing can confirm a relationship.

### Where can I find my ancestors' birth, marriage and death records?

Older parish books and civil registration records are kept by the state archives, diocesan archives and parishes. Many have scans on Szukajwarchiwach, and their indexes are often in Geneteka. Records from recent decades are held by civil registry offices.

### Does Name Atlas show my family tree?

No. Name Atlas shows facts about a surname: how many people bear it, how rare it is, where it occurs and how that has changed over time. It does not connect people into families and does not guess where your ancestors came from.
