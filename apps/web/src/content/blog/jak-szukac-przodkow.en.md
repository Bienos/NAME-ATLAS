Our [first post](/en/blog/genealogia) listed the sources worth starting with. This one shows how to use them in order, from a drawer at home to the scan of a record a hundred years old. You can do most of the steps online.

## Step 1: gather what you have at home

Archivists advise starting with your own family papers. Useful items include birth, baptism and marriage certificates, school reports, ID cards, military and work documents, property deeds, newspaper cuttings such as obituaries, diaries, and prayer books with dedications. Put what you find in order straight away, for example as a family tree, and check it in conversations with older relatives.

Magdalena Masłowska of the State Archive in Warsaw said in 2020 that almost everyone who starts looking for their roots says they know nothing. To begin with, a great-grandfather's first name and the name of his village or parish are enough.

## Step 2: work out the parish and district

Place names in Poland can repeat, so you need to establish which parish, commune (gmina) and district (powiat) your ancestors' village belonged to. Borders of states, offices and parishes changed many times, so it helps to learn the region's history. This matters in practice: archives keep documents according to where and by whom they were created. Once you know who wrote a record and where, it is easier to work out where it is kept today.

## Step 3: search the indexes in Geneteka

[Geneteka](https://geneteka.genealodzy.pl/) is the Polish Genealogical Society's database of indexes to parish registers. A search result shows the year and record number, the first name and surname, both parents' first names, the mother's surname, the parish and the place. The year, parish and record number are enough to find the record itself later.

Search several versions of the surname, including the older women's forms with -owa and -ówna that our [post on surname endings](/en/blog/koncowki-nazwisk) explains. The parents' names help tell apart two people with the same first name and surname.

## Step 4: look at the scan of the record

[Szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl](https://www.szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl/) is the portal of the Polish State Archives, run by the National Digital Archive. You can browse scans of parish books and civil registration records there for free. Look for the records of the parish or civil registry office for your ancestors' village in the archive that covers that area, and open the book for the year the index gave.

You may not be able to read every record at once. Most documents a family historian works with are handwritten. In the Kingdom of Poland, from 1 January 1868, civil registration books for Catholics were kept in Russian and Polish, and those for other faiths in Russian only. Some entries are also in Latin.

## Step 5: newer records are at the civil registry office

Not every record is in an archive yet. Under Poland's 2014 Civil Status Records Act, a civil registry office keeps birth records for 100 years and marriage and death records for 80 years, counted from the end of the year in which they were made. Only then, within two years, does it pass them to the state archive. So a grandfather's birth record from the 1930s will usually still be at the registry office.

Copies of records are issued, among others, to the person the record concerns, their spouse, ascendants, descendants and siblings, and to anyone who shows a legal interest. Grandchildren and great-grandchildren are descendants, so they can ask for a copy of a grandparent's or great-grandparent's record.

## Step 6: when there is no index, write to the archive

Not every book has an index, and not every book has been scanned. In that case you can send an enquiry to a specific archive about the documents you are looking for. Masłowska said the state archives have consultants ready to help with this. Parishes and church archives also keep parish books and records.

## Where Name Atlas helps

Name Atlas does not search for ancestors. It shows facts about a surname: how many people bear it today according to the PESEL register, how rare it is, and in which voivodeships it is most common. It does not tell you where your ancestors came from, because for that you need the records. [Look up your surname](/?lang=en).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does it cost anything to search for ancestors online?

Browsing scans on Szukajwarchiwach is free. A copy of a record from a civil registry office and an enquiry you commission from an archive may cost money, so ask the office or the archive about its terms.

### Why is my grandfather's birth record not in the archive?

If the record was made less than 100 years ago, it is usually still at the civil registry office. Marriage and death records go to the archive after 80 years.

### Who can get a copy of a record from a civil registry office?

Among others, the person the record concerns, their spouse, ascendants, descendants and siblings, and anyone who shows a legal interest.

### What if the record is in Russian or Latin?

Start with what you can read without knowing the language: the date, the record number and the names. Archive staff sometimes help with reading handwritten documents, and Masłowska mentioned consultants at the state archives.

### How do I tell my ancestor apart from someone with the same surname?

Compare the parents' names, the mother's surname, the parish and the dates. Geneteka shows both parents' first names and the mother's surname, so you can often check this in the index. The surname alone is not enough, because it could have arisen in many unrelated families.
