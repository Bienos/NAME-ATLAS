Today everyone has a surname, gets it at birth and carries it on their ID card. For most of Polish history it was not like that. This post tells how one name became two, when a surname began to pass from father to children, and how it found its way into records, statutes and finally the PESEL register.

## In the beginning there was one name

The linguist Ewa Wolnicz-Pawłowska points to the Bull of Gniezno of 1136, one of the oldest documents about Poland. Each person in it has one name. These were two-part names such as Chwalibor or Mirosław, short forms of them such as Miłosz or Radosz, and names that were ordinary words, such as Broda ("beard"), Kwiatek ("little flower") or Kobyłka ("little mare"). There were few people, living in small villages and towns, so one name was usually enough. The rest came from the document's context: the place, the office, the trade.

After the Council of Trent (1545-1563) children were given saints' names at baptism, and the old Slavic names fell out of use as first names. Many of them survived in surnames. We write more about Slavic names in our [post on first names and surnames](/en/blog/imie-i-nazwisko-ciekawostki).

## Bynames: son, village, nickname, trade

From the thirteenth century, documents more and more often add descriptions to a person's name. Wolnicz-Pawłowska gives examples: Clemens filius Marcii, Clement son of Mark; Niegoslaw Janowic, son of Jan; Sulisław Gamba, nicknamed Gęba ("mouth"). The more important someone was, the more such descriptions were written beside their name.

Over time the long descriptions shrank to a single word. Instead of "Nawój of Morawica" the record says Nawój Morawicki; instead of "Jan, son of Sulisław", Jan Sulisławic. That is how four main groups arose, still visible in surnames today:

- from the place a person came from or owned, like Baranowski, "of Baranów",
- from the father or the kin, like Janiak or Piotrowczyk,
- from a trait, like Kopyto ("hoof") or Trąba ("trumpet"),
- from a trade, like Kowal ("smith") or Tkacz ("weaver").

A byname was not yet a surname. It did not have to pass to the children and could change during a person's life.

## The nobility and names from estates

The nobility were the first to use fixed surnames. Zofia Kaleta, author of a monograph on Polish surnames, finds that bynames began to work as family names mainly in the fourteenth century, first among the emerging nobility and later among townspeople. Wolnicz-Pawłowska writes that from the second half of the fifteenth century surnames clearly settle: one person uses the same name all the time, and the name covers the whole family. The nobility named themselves mostly after the villages and towns they held, hence the Zamojskis, Koniecpolskis and Tarnowskis. Townspeople and peasants used names from their fathers' first names and from nicknames.

## The fashion for -ski

By the sixteenth century a surname in -ski was seen as the better kind. Wolnicz-Pawłowska gives the example of a nobleman recorded in 1576 as Skowron who appears in the following years as Skowroński. Likewise, Czajka changed his name to Czajkowski. Such changes were very common, especially among the petty nobility. People outside the nobility took -ski names too, and the law had no penalty for it.

The nobility found this irritating. One nobleman, Walerian Nekanda-Trepka, listed over 2,500 cases in a book known as *Liber chamorum*, the "Book of Boors": townspeople and peasants who in his view passed themselves off as nobles, often by taking a -ski surname. The book was published only after the Second World War. It did not stop the trend. The surname dictionary of the Institute of the Polish Language (IJP PAN) writes that a large share of -ski surnames were formed, from the sixteenth century on, from ordinary words, on the model of surnames from place names, which were already seen as "better".

So the -ski ending does not prove noble descent. Many such surnames do not come from any village at all. We write more about endings in our [post on -ski, -wicz, -ak and -uk](/en/blog/koncowki-nazwisk).

## Townspeople and peasants: later, and not all at once

A fixed surname came in turn: first to the nobility, then to townspeople, last to peasants. Wolnicz-Pawłowska notes that some peasant surnames arose only in the nineteenth century, as did, for example, Jewish surnames. Even in the nineteenth century, among townspeople and peasants, the same person, or a father and son, could be recorded under different forms of one surname, for example Szulowski, Sulowski and Siulowski.

Other languages shaped surnames too. Under Ukrainian and Belarusian influence the old Polish ending -ic, -owic came to be pronounced and written -icz, -owicz. The old form survives in a few names, such as Staszic. According to Wolnicz-Pawłowska, the surnames Mickiewicz (from Mićko, that is Mikołaj) and Sienkiewicz (from Sieńko, that is Symeon) are Belarusian in origin.

Women's surnames had forms of their own. Małgorzata Kowalska, who studied the nineteenth-century parish registers of Pisarzowa, records the suffixes -owa, -ina, -ówna and -anka, such as Kowalczykowa or Wróblówna. Such forms could mean either a wife or a daughter. In today's dialects -owa and -ina are falling out of use.

That matters for anyone tracing a family. In old registers an ancestor's surname may be spelled several ways, women often appear under a form in -owa or -ówna, and in the oldest registers the surname may be missing altogether.

## The partitions and civil registration

Before that, baptisms and marriages were recorded by parishes. In November 1563 the Council of Trent ordered parish priests to keep registers. In Poland, diocesan synods confirmed the duty until the end of the 1570s, and the synod of Piotrków extended it to the whole Commonwealth.

Kaleta points out that a legal duty to carry a hereditary surname came to the Polish lands at the end of the eighteenth century, through the laws of Austria, Prussia and Russia after the partitions. From then on a surname was a matter of law, not only of custom.

The best-documented rules concern Jews, among whom inherited surnames were not common until the end of the eighteenth century:

- in the Austrian partition, the patents of Emperor Joseph II in 1787 required Jews to take a fixed surname within two years,
- in South Prussia and New East Prussia, the general statute for Jews of 17 April 1797 obliged them to take surnames,
- in the Kingdom of Poland, a decision of the viceroy in March 1821 gave every Jew six months to declare at an office the first name and surname they would use, unchanged, from then on.

In the Duchy of Warsaw the turning point was the Napoleonic Code. The legal historian Stanisław Grodziski quotes the decree of 27 January 1808 that brought it in from 1 May 1808. Civil registration came with the code. Because there were not enough officials, parish priests were made civil registrars. That is why many nineteenth-century civil records were written by a priest acting as a state official. We cover this in our [post on the history of genealogy](/en/blog/historia-genealogii) too, and how to find such records today in our [guide to finding ancestors](/en/blog/jak-szukac-przodkow).

## Surnames in law: changing one, and marriage

After the war, changing a surname was first governed by the decree of 10 November 1945 on changing and establishing first names and surnames. Later in communist Poland the Act of 15 November 1956 on changing first names and surnames applied. In the wording in force until 2008, a surname could be changed for important reasons, among them a surname that was ridiculous or offended human dignity, sounded non-Polish, or had the form of a first name. In that wording the act also said a surname could have at most two parts.

Today the Act of 17 October 2008 on changing a first name and surname applies. It too requires important reasons and lists, among others, a ridiculous surname or one that offends human dignity, a change to the surname a person actually uses, and a return to a surname changed unlawfully. A non-Polish sound is no longer on the list. The decision is made by the head of the civil registry office.

A surname also changes at marriage. What the Family and Guardianship Code says about that, and why Polish surnames have a male and a female form, is in our [post on Kowalski and Kowalska](/en/blog/kowalski-czy-kowalska).

## PESEL: the surname in a register

The last chapter of the story is a register. Sylwia Czubkowska described on the Forsal news site how the Universal Electronic System for Registration of the Population (PESEL) was built. In June 1970 the government's presidium decided to prepare its concept, and an act of 1974 set the system's start for 1979. As a pilot, residents of Warsaw's Wola district received the first identity cards with PESEL numbers at the end of the 1970s, and all citizens had been given numbers by 1984. The state IT centre, Centralny Ośrodek Informatyki, says the central PESEL database went into service on 1 July 1985.

Today the Ministry of Digital Affairs calls the PESEL register the most important register of personal and address data in the state. According to its description the register holds, among other things, the surname, the surname at birth, and the parents' first names and surnames at birth. From this register the ministry publishes lists of living people's surnames on dane.gov.pl. The lists count the current surname and leave out surnames with a single occurrence and data on people who have died.

So the list shows who carries a surname today. It does not say when or where the surname arose.

## What this means for your surname

The history of surnames tells you what kinds of surnames arose, when and in which groups. It does not tell you when your family took its surname. A -ski surname may come from a village or be modelled on surnames from villages. A surname from a first name may have arisen in many places independently. A peasant family may have had a fixed surname for a long time or only from the nineteenth century.

The answer for a particular family is in the records: parish registers, civil records and censuses. What a surname alone can and cannot tell you is in our [post on what a surname says](/en/blog/co-mozna-wyczytac-z-nazwiska), and why people look for their ancestors at all is in the [blog's first post](/en/blog/genealogia).

## Frequently asked questions

### Since when have Poles had surnames?

Bynames beside first names appear in documents in large numbers from the thirteenth century. Kaleta finds that they began to work as family names mainly in the fourteenth century, among the nobility. Townspeople and peasants got fixed surnames later, some only in the nineteenth century.

### Does a surname ending in -ski mean noble descent?

No. From the sixteenth century people outside the nobility took -ski surnames too, and many were formed from ordinary words, not from village names. A family's origin is confirmed by the records, not by an ending.

### Why is my ancestor's surname spelled differently in old records?

Because even in the nineteenth century the surnames of townspeople and peasants had no single fixed form. The same person could be written as Szulowski, Sulowski or Siulowski. Search for every variant.

### Why does a woman's surname end in -owa or -ówna in old registers?

In nineteenth-century registers women's surnames often had forms of their own: -owa, -ina, -ówna or -anka, such as Kowalczykowa or Wróblówna. They could mean either a wife or a daughter. When looking for a female ancestor, check these forms alongside her husband's or father's surname.

### When did civil registration begin?

In the Duchy of Warsaw, with the Napoleonic Code, in force from 1 May 1808. Before that, parishes recorded baptisms, marriages and deaths in their registers, as we describe in our [post on the history of genealogy](/en/blog/historia-genealogii).

### Does the PESEL register say where a surname comes from?

No. The lists from the PESEL register show how many living people carry a surname today. Where a surname comes from is a question for linguists' dictionaries and old sources.
