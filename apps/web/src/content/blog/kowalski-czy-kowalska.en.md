Jan Kowalski has a sister, Anna Kowalska. Jan Nowak has a sister, Anna Nowak. Two surnames, two families and two different rules. In Polish that is nothing unusual, but it can surprise foreigners, and sometimes Poles too. Where does the difference come from, and what does it mean when you look up your surname?

## Surnames that are adjectives

Surnames ending in -ski, -cki and -dzki are built like adjectives, the same as the words *polski* (Polish) or *miejski* (urban). A Polish adjective changes form with grammatical gender, which is why a man is Kowalski and a woman Kowalska, Zawadzki and Zawadzka, Kozłowski and Kozłowska. These are not two surnames but one surname in two forms.

Why there are so many surnames ending in -ski is explained in [our piece on surname endings](/en/blog/koncowki-nazwisk).

## Surnames that are nouns

Nowak, Wójcik, Kowalczyk and Mazur are nouns. A noun has no feminine form the way an adjective does, so for centuries women were marked differently: with a suffix. Nowak's wife was Nowakowa, and his unmarried daughter Nowakówna. The suffix told you not only that the person was a woman but also whether she was married. More on these old forms, and how they help you find female ancestors in old registers, is in [our piece on endings](/en/blog/koncowki-nazwisk).

## A 1951 argument, and one form wins

In the 20th century the custom began to fade, and linguists argued about what to do with it. Tadeusz Lewaszkiewicz of Adam Mickiewicz University recalls a debate from 1951. Eugeniusz Pawłowski then held that the only correct female forms of noun surnames were those with the suffixes -owa, -ówna, -ina and -anka. Kazimierz Nitsch was more liberal: if a first name or another word showing that the person is a woman stands beside the surname, no suffix can be compulsory, and it should all depend on the person's own wish.

As Lewaszkiewicz writes, women's surnames with suffixes are judged outdated today, so one can speak of a victory of the masculine forms. That is why today we say "Ms Anna Nowak", while "Nowakowa" sounds like something from an old novel.

## Your surname after marriage

What someone is called after marriage is no longer decided by an ending but by a declaration. Article 25 of the Family and Guardianship Code lets spouses:

- share one surname, the existing surname of one of them,
- each keep their own existing surname,
- add the other spouse's surname to their own.

A combined surname may have no more than two parts, for example Kowalska-Nowak. If the spouses make no declaration, each keeps their existing surname. A woman who takes an adjective surname usually bears it in the feminine form: Anna Nowak, marrying Jan Kowalski, may become Anna Kowalska.

## One surname, two lists

This double form shows in the data too. The Ministry of Digital Affairs publishes the surnames in the PESEL register as two separate lists: men's surnames and women's surnames. Kowalski is on the men's list, Kowalska on the women's. Nowak is on both, spelled the same.

Name Atlas shows the two lists side by side, each with its own count, place in the ranking and date, and does not add them together. That way each figure comes straight from one published list. When a surname has a second form, we show it too, for example Kowalski and Kowalska, but as a form of the language. It is not proof that people with these surnames are related. ## Frequently asked questions

### Why is a woman called Kowalska and not Kowalski?

Because Kowalski is built like an adjective, and a Polish adjective changes form with grammatical gender. It is one surname in two forms.

### Why is a woman called Nowak and not Nowakowa?

Nowak is a noun surname. Women once bore it with the suffix -owa (wife) or -ówna (daughter), but today these forms are considered outdated and the basic form is used.

### Can you have two surnames after marriage?

Yes. The Family and Guardianship Code lets you add your spouse's surname to your own, but a combined surname may have no more than two parts.

### Are Kowalski and Kowalska the same family?

Not necessarily. They are two forms of the same surname, but the surname Kowalski arose independently in many families. Only records or DNA tests confirm kinship.
