A surname page on Name Atlas has a map of Poland's voivodeships. Some are brighter, some darker, and some are empty. It is easy to read it as an answer to "where does my family come from?". The map does not answer that question. This post explains what it does show and how to use it without reading more into it than it holds.

## What the map shows

The map in the chapter "Where is it most often registered?" is built from the PESEL register's surname lists, which the Ministry of Digital Affairs publishes on dane.gov.pl. One of these lists splits a surname's bearers by the voivodeship of their permanent registered residence. The map we show is as of 20 January 2026.

A few things follow directly from the publisher's description of the data:

- **These are living people.** The publisher says data on deceased people was not included.
- **This is the current surname.** The list counts people by the surname they carry today. A woman who changed her surname at marriage is counted under the new one, not her maiden name.
- **Men and women are on separate lists.** That is why a surname page has two maps, and we never add their figures together. The [post on Kowalski and Kowalska](/en/blog/kowalski-czy-kowalska) explains why.
- **These are shares, not every bearer.** A voivodeship's colour is its share among the people with this surname who have a voivodeship of registration on the list. The page says so under the map: shares are of the people with a registered voivodeship, not of every bearer of the surname. So the number of people beside the map is often smaller than the one in the chapter "How many people have this surname?". The voivodeship list does not cover everyone on the national list, and the publisher does not say why.

## Registered residence is not origin

Poland's Act on Population Records defines permanent residence as living in a given locality at a stated address with the intention of staying permanently. A person can have only one place of permanent residence at a time. It is an address written in the register. It need not be where someone actually lives, let alone where their grandparents came from.

Under the map we put it in one sentence: a permanent registered residence is an address in the register, not an actual address, and not information about a family's roots. A bright voivodeship says only that a relatively large number of people with this surname are registered there today. It does not say that the surname arose there or that your family came from there.

## Today, not the past

The map is a picture of a single day. The chapter "How did this count change between editions of the list?" shows how the number of bearers in the whole country has changed since 2019, but not where they lived. About where a family lived a hundred years ago, the map says nothing.

Even the voivodeships themselves are recent. The sixteen you see on the map were created on 1 January 1999. Before that, from 1975, Poland had 49 voivodeships. The records you search for ancestors often name units that no longer exist and borders that ran differently.

The country's own borders changed too. After the Second World War, at the Potsdam Conference in 1945, the former German lands east of the Oder and the Western Neisse were placed under the administration of the Polish state, and the German population remaining in Poland was to be transferred to Germany. Poland's Institute of National Remembrance describes how the authorities brought into the new borders Poles who had ended up outside the country because the borders moved, among them people from the former Eastern Borderlands. A family registered today in Lower Silesia or West Pomerania may have its roots hundreds of kilometres further east.

## People move

Statistics Poland (GUS) describes internal migration as changes of residence within the country, and counts inter-voivodeship migration, moves from one voivodeship to another, separately. In its statistics, a permanent-residence migration is a move tied to registering a permanent residence. Each such move shifts someone on a surname's map.

So the map is the sum of many decisions over many generations: about work, study, marriage, housing. When a surname is common today in a large city, that may mean no more than that many people moved there.

## Empty voivodeships

Some voivodeships on the map are empty and marked "not listed in the dataset — not zero". You will see them most often with rare surnames.

The publisher says that surnames occurring only once were not included. In the voivodeship files we read, the smallest number in a single voivodeship is 2. If a voivodeship had just one person with a surname, that voivodeship simply does not appear on the list.

## Why missing is not zero

An empty voivodeship can mean three different things:

- nobody with this surname is registered there,
- one person is registered there, and the list does not show them,
- someone with this surname is on the national list but in no row of the voivodeship list, so where they are registered is unknown.

The data alone cannot tell which of these is true. That is why we never write that a surname is "not found" in a voivodeship, and we advise against reading the map that way. An empty field is missing information, not information that nobody is there.

## How to use the map as a hint

The map can help if you treat it as a question, not an answer.

1. **Start with your family, not the map.** First gather what you know at home: certificates, birthplaces, parishes. The [post on finding your ancestors](/en/blog/jak-szukac-przodkow) shows how.
2. **Check the date and the scope.** The map covers living people, their current surname and their voivodeship of permanent registered residence, as of 20 January 2026. Every figure on it holds only within that scope.
3. **Compare, carefully.** If your records lead to an area and the surname is often registered there today, that is a good reason to keep searching there. It does not prove that the other bearers are your relatives. The [post on whether people with the same surname are related](/en/blog/czy-to-samo-nazwisko-to-rodzina) explains why.
4. **Draw nothing from empty fields.** An empty voivodeship does not rule out that someone in your family lives or lived there.
5. **Look for confirmation in the records.** Where a family lived in the past is shown by parish registers, civil registration records and censuses, not by a list from 2026.

How to read the other chapters of a surname page is in our [step-by-step guide](/en/blog/jak-sprawdzic-pochodzenie-nazwiska), and what else a surname can and cannot tell you is in the [post on what a surname reveals](/en/blog/co-mozna-wyczytac-z-nazwiska).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the map show where my family comes from?

No. It shows the voivodeships where people with this surname had their permanent registered residence on 20 January 2026. That is today's state, not a family's origin.

### Why is my voivodeship empty on the map when I live there?

The list leaves out single occurrences, and not every bearer has a voivodeship of registration on it. An empty field can also mean you are registered somewhere other than where you live. That is why absence from the dataset is not zero.

### Does the map include people who have died?

No. The publisher left data on deceased people out of the list, so the map covers living people only.

### Are women counted under their maiden name?

No. The list counts the current surname. A woman who changed her surname at marriage is counted under the surname she carries today.

### Can I compare today's map with one from a hundred years ago?

Not from this data. The PESEL list covers living people, and today's voivodeships have existed since 1999. Records, not the map, tell you about places in the past.
