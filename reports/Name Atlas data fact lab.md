# Official open data proves counts, not origins

Name Atlas can already publish a wide range of defensible surname facts from official open files, and the safest are among the most engaging: per-list PESEL counts and ranks, a 2019–2026 trend, how the name declines in Polish, whether it is also an ordinary word, letter trivia, and how often the same spelling appears in the German casualty lists of 1914–1919. A fact lab computed 27 fact types for real surnames from the Ministry of Digital Affairs' PESEL files, the SGJP and sjp.pl dictionaries, the official PRNG gazetteer, the US Census and a CC0 First World War dataset, and a demo builder that recomputed the showcase figures matched the analysts' tables in 151 of 152 cross-checks. The real risks are wording and method, not arithmetic: the regional and diaspora analysts' own headline sentences add the male and female lists together, which AC-17 forbids, and every regional fact, including the "where is my surname common" answer users keep asking for, waits on the open adoption and licence decision D-004. Shares, 1-in-X and rarity wait on D-006, gender ratios on U-1, and meaning, origin and the causes of any change stay INSUFFICIENT_DATA. The build order follows from those gates: national per-list facts and aggregate editorial cards first; the casualty-list tile, the share of deceased-person records, official place-name matches and US counts as each clears one check; maps and then-versus-now comparisons only after D-004 and per-list reruns. Every fact needs its source file, reference date, method tag and version, and an explicit "fewer than 2 entries or absent" state, because the files contain a duplicated year, a discontinuous first snapshot, placeholder "surnames", a spreadsheet artefact that turned PRAWDA into "True", and about a fifth of each list's entries missing from the voivodeship breakdown.

## Twenty-seven fact types compute today, and their surface sets the wording

The fact lab worked in three families. The national family (A1–A11) reads the PESEL lists of living persons, dataset 1681, at eight reference dates from 30 June 2019 to 20 January 2026, and the list that also includes deceased persons, dataset 568 ([dataset 1681](https://api.dane.gov.pl/1.4/datasets/1681); [dataset 568](https://api.dane.gov.pl/1.4/datasets/568)). The regional family (B1–B8, plus B2b) reads the 2025 and 2026 lists broken down by voivodeship of permanent registered residence, together with the official PRNG gazetteer of localities ([voivodeship male list, 20 Jan 2026](https://api.dane.gov.pl/media/resources/20260128/nazwiska_m%C4%99skie-osoby_%C5%BCyj%C4%85ce_w_podziale_na_wojew%C3%B3dztwo_zameldowania.csv); [female list](https://api.dane.gov.pl/media/resources/20260128/nazwiska_%C5%BCe%C5%84skie-osoby_%C5%BCyj%C4%85ce_w_podziale_na_wojew%C3%B3dztwo_zameldowania.csv); [PRNG, GUGiK](https://opendata.geoportal.gov.pl/prng/PRNG_MIEJSCOWOSCI_XLSX.zip)). The diaspora and history family (C1–C7) adds the US Census surname files for 2000 and 2020 and the Verein für Computergenealogie's German casualty lists, **8,551,758 list entries** released under CC0 ([Census 2020](https://www.census.gov/topics/population/genealogy/data/2020_names.html); [Census 2000](https://www.census.gov/topics/population/genealogy/data/2000_surnames.html); [casualty lists, Zenodo](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18330751)). Language facts come from the SGJP inflection data (BSD-2) and the sjp.pl word list ([SGJP licence](https://morfeusz.sgjp.pl/doc/license/); [sjp.pl](https://sjp.pl/sl/odmiany/)). A script printed every figure from files pinned by SHA-256 checksum, and every PESEL figure is stated per list: the male form in the male list and the female form in the female list, side by side, never added, ranked together or compared. D-006 leaves open whether one entry equals one person, and AC-17 forbids summing, merging or comparing the lists.

The fact types are easiest to judge rendered. The private demo page [Name Atlas Fact Lab](https://claude.ai/artifact/45YwYRJmYgvfuMg5pUsotQ) covers 12 surnames (Nowak, Kowalski, Wiśniewski, Kaczmarek, Mazur, Szczepański, Kozioł, Grzegorczyk, Bartkowiak, Bagiński, Formela and Czekierda) with one chapter per fact type, running from "In the register today" and "Since 2019" through "Where entries are registered", "A place with the same adjective", "Saying it", "Also a word", "Living and deceased" and "Abroad" to "A century ago". Eight cross-surname stories follow: "More names, not more people", "The biggest names", "A calendar of surnames", "Regional favourites", "Endings on the map", "Place and surname", "Across the Atlantic" and "A century apart". Its builder recomputed every figure from the official files and ran **152 cross-checks against the analysts' tables, of which 151 matched**. The one mismatch (SZCZEPAŃSKI, 17.0% against 16.9%) is a method difference: the builder kept the exact spelling, while the analyst pooled it with the no-diacritics spelling SZCZEPANSKI. The demo also models the per-list pattern the product needs. Its regional chapter draws separate male-list and female-list maps behind a two-state toggle that fits a phone screen, where the regional analyst had used summed M+F totals.

*Update, 29 September 2026: the demo has since been republished in Polish at the same link, as "Laboratorium faktów Name Atlas". The four copy slips described below are fixed there. The kicker now reads "Więcej form, niewiele więcej wpisów", the uncited -ski generalisation, the unsourced declension line and the word gloss are gone, and near-ties now use a 15% or fewer-than-100-entries rule. The regional chapter states that its product use waits on D-004. The English chapter names in this report refer to the first version.*

Labels follow one logic across all three families.

| Kind of statement | Label | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| A direct reading of a file | **VERIFIED** | A count, a set difference, a dictionary listing, a string property |
| A value that depends on method choices; it stores its method tag and version | **LIKELY** | A share, a location quotient (LQ: a surname's rate in a voivodeship divided by its national rate), a trend word, a permutation-test result |
| A link between one surname and one place, spelling or century-old pattern | **POSSIBLE** at most | A candidate place from the gazetteer |

### Surname page: two columns of counts, grammar and one historical tile

Apart from the regional card, the free page can answer four questions without touching any open decision: how many, since when, how the name is written and declined, and whether the spelling was recorded a century ago. The core card reads the exact form in each list, the -SKA form for a -SKI surname, and shows both lists on one scale ([male list, 20 Jan 2026](https://api.dane.gov.pl/media/resources/20260128/2_-_nazwiska_m%C4%99skie-osoby_%C5%BCyj%C4%85ce.csv); [female list, 20 Jan 2026](https://api.dane.gov.pl/media/resources/20260128/2_-_nazwiska_%C5%BCe%C5%84skie-osoby_%C5%BCyj%C4%85ce.csv)). Entries written in the other list's form go in a footnote, such as the 535 female-list entries written KOWALSKI. The first pairing rule (v1) pairs only -SKI/-CKI/-DZKI with -SKA/-CKA/-DZKA. A KONIECZNY page therefore shows the female-list form KONIECZNY (2,503 entries) and treats KONIECZNA (11,693) as a separate unit until pairing is extended. KRASKA, a noun-type surname with 2,446 male-list entries, is never "the female form of KRASKI". All examples below come from the fact-lab tables ([showcase_surnames.csv](../research_notes/Name%20Atlas%20data%20fact%20lab/data/national/showcase_surnames.csv); [showcase_regional.csv](../research_notes/Name%20Atlas%20data%20fact%20lab/data/regional/showcase_regional.csv); [showcase_wwi_summary.csv](../research_notes/Name%20Atlas%20data%20fact%20lab/data/diaspora_history/showcase_wwi_summary.csv)).

### Editorial and social: aggregates travel, ranked lists of forms do not

Editorial surfaces get the most striking national numbers. The rule that separates safe cards from unsafe ones is simple: aggregates travel, ranked lists of individual forms do not. "+47% distinct forms against +7% entries" and "no form with 20 or more entries has left the published list since 2019" are fully sourced, and they counter the "dying surname" myth ([male list, 30 Jun 2019](https://api.dane.gov.pl/media/resources/20190813/nazwiska_m%C4%99skie.csv); [male list, 20 Jan 2026](https://api.dane.gov.pl/media/resources/20260128/2_-_nazwiska_m%C4%99skie-osoby_%C5%BCyj%C4%85ce.csv)). Three kinds of list are arithmetically correct but unsafe: the fastest-rising forms, newly published forms, and forms with no deceased-person records. A few spelling patterns dominate them (MELNYK, SHEVCHENKO, KOVALENKO; TKACH at 0.00%), so they read as origin stories by juxtaposition. That is how Forsal framed its 2023 story "Melnyk goni Kaczyńskiego" ([Forsal](https://forsal.pl/gospodarka/demografia/artykuly/8650868,najpopularniejsze-nazwiska-w-polsce-melnyk-goni-kaczynskiego.html)). Every list applies the 20-entry editorial floor per list (ELIG-V1). Applying it to summed lists instead had admitted forms with fewer than 20 entries in each list, and moving to the per-list rule cut eligible units from 136,968 to 103,330.

### Premium report: depth where the surname has inventory

The premium report sells depth: the full per-list series, the regional table, candidate places with coordinates, the per-voivodeship historical table with printed places and spelling chips, the then-versus-now comparison, US counts with similar spellings, and the methodology appendix. PRODUCT §6 offers a report only when a surname has real inventory, and the fact lab shows how uneven inventory is. KACZMAREK has a strong layer in every family. GRZEGORCZYK has only 43 casualty-list entries, and its 2026 top voivodeship is Lubelskie, which lay outside the German Empire in 1914. CZEKIERDA has no historical or diaspora layer at all. The US figures come from files with no stated licence, so the diaspora module also waits on a terms check.

## Build first: national per-list facts clear every open gate

Priority follows four tests: the source is pinned or easy to pin, the label reaches VERIFIED, no open decision blocks the fact, and the notes rate it highly. The national per-list set passes all four. Dataset 1681's 2026 national resources are already pinned (D-001), TASK-004 already stores their raw counts, and the earlier snapshots come from the same CC0 dataset. The launch set answers two requests that users often send to the IJP PAN surname dictionary, up-to-date counts and coverage of rare surnames, for every form with at least two entries in a list ([Górny 2024](https://onomastica.ijppan.pl/index.php/ONOM/article/view/533/730)). It cannot yet answer "which parts of Poland is it common in", the request behind the regional card, because D-004 is open. That makes the D-004 decision the most valuable non-code task on the list.

The demo builder's checks show why these rules must live in shared code rather than in each analyst's script. Working independently, the builder hit the same NA/NULL loading pitfall the diaspora analyst had fixed, and its lone mismatch came from a silent variant-pooling choice. The demo rehearses the display rules well. It keeps blanks as "Not listed — not zero", suppresses MAZUR's "also a word" line, shows "not found" for CZEKIERDA's casualty-list search and drops the US-to-PESEL ratio. It also shows how easily copy drifts, with four slips to fix before any of it is reused. One story kicker reads "More names, not more people", although PESEL counts are entries. The place chapter opens with an uncited generalisation, "Many -ski surnames were formed from place names", of the kind the B8 rule forbids. The declension note says families "usually keep the one closest to the written surname", which the notes rate INSUFFICIENT_DATA for any given family. Second, the page computes trend percentages and tie flags in browser script, which ARCHITECTURE §6 reserves for validated claims computed upstream. Its "more entries than X% of the forms in this list" line is a rarity context, which the open denominator question in D-006 still governs.

## Every fact travels the same six-step claim chain

The notes' method tags map directly onto the project's claim chain, Source → SourceRecord → EvidenceItem → Claim → ConfidenceAssessment → Citation, with three design consequences. First, each PESEL list is its own surname-scoped DistributionSnapshot in its own comparability group, so no total can be formed by accident. The voivodeship totals that serve as LQ denominators are register-scoped and must not be modelled as a DistributionSnapshot. Second, derived values are claims: rank, share, LQ, tie flag, deceased-record share and persistence overlap each carry a method tag and version (RANK-V1, REGIONAL-v1, TIE-v1, AGE-V1, persist-v1). They are never bare columns and never computed in the page. Third, absence is an explicit state. A missing PESEL row is "fewer than 2 entries or absent", a missing US name "not listed (<100 or absent)", and a missing casualty-list spelling "not found in the transcribed lists".

The lab's clearest CONFLICTING case is NOWAK's word status. The sjp.pl word list has no lowercase base form "nowak", while SGJP lists a common noun *nowak*. Two sources contradict each other, so a word claim for NOWAK would be CONFLICTING. The notes recommend making no claim at all.

Rights decide where a fact may appear, never how true it is (ADR-0001). US 2020 counts are VERIFIED readings, but the file states no licence, and a commercial-use status of UNKNOWN blocks paid surfaces, so the premium "abroad" module waits on a terms check. The 2026 voivodeship counts are VERIFIED readings too, yet no surface may show them until D-004 closes. PRNG facts need a GUGiK credit wherever they appear, SGJP its BSD-2 notice and sjp.pl its credit. None of this changes a label.

Because claim prose is generated from structured values, the wording rules are testable. The EN and PL templates in the notes can sit behind a linter that rejects "people", "osób" or "ludzi" next to a PESEL count, a plus sign between list values, origin verbs beside place or region facts, and any count shown without its scope and date.

## Conclusion

The fact lab shifts where the difficulty lies. It is not in computing numbers, since 151 of 152 independent checks agreed, but in three disciplines the numbers do not enforce: keeping the lists separate, versioning variant handling and controlling wording. The single mismatch is the product thesis in miniature. Pooling the no-diacritics SZCZEPANSKI (39 male-list entries nationally) with SZCZEPAŃSKI (16,419) moved a share by a tenth of a point. Earlier competitor research found surname.pl making the same pooling choice silently, showing 16,458 under an unexplained "2 variants" ([surname.pl](https://surname.pl/szczepanski)). In Name Atlas, that choice becomes a named method with a version and a disclosed effect. The analysts' own user-facing drafts summed the lists in two of three families, and the demo's copy slipped "people" into a headline, so these rules belong in contracts, loaders and a template linter rather than in reviewers' memory.

The sequencing consequence is just as concrete. The richest free-page promise, current geography and then-versus-now, depends on one internal decision, while the most personal questions (how many people, how rare, where from) depend on publisher answers or licensed scholarship. The launch page will therefore be honest but uneven: full for a KACZMAREK, and compressed for a CZEKIERDA to counts, grammar, a trend and a candid "not found". That unevenness is the visible proof that the inventory is truthful. Showing the lists separately also costs rare surnames more. Halving the counts pushes more voivodeship cells below the publication threshold: in the demo, FORMELA's male-list map loses six voivodeships where the summed figures lost four. Under AC-17, small-number rules matter more, not less.
